## Applied conventions

1. **F-levels under amendment v2.1.** F3 needs attribution sentences (naming a paper, agency, company or person) in the output of at least two separately worded queries, naming at least two sources of different authorship, with at least one primary domain independent of whoever produced the claim. Press-office releases, wire copies, indexing abstracts, ResearchGate or Semantic Scholar copies, and news rewrites count as one origin with the study. **No W7 figure reached F3.** Single-study figures are F2 even when they recur across many queries. An advocacy organisation's claim about its own programme, and an organisation's poll of its own members, are V2-P.
2. **Leading queries.** Several later queries repeated a figure already seen in earlier output, and Q-18 carried two figures from background memory (56% and 38%). Repeats that echo a figure supplied in the query are not counted as independent confirmation (flagged in Section 7).
3. **Tiers** follow `cross_agent_quality_definitions.md` (tier_1 top-quartile peer-reviewed, tier_2 other peer-reviewed, tier_3 academic not peer-reviewed, tier_4 grey literature). Journal quartile was not checked this session, so Tier 1 for Health Affairs, Nature Aging and the Journal of Political Economy is provisional.
4. **Origin classes** in the search log: PUB publisher or journal; PM PubMed, PMC or Europe PMC; PRE preprint server; FND survey organisation publishing its own data (Pew); GOV government or international body; PO university or institutional news office; WIRE press-release wire; NEWS news or magazine; BLOG blog or personal site; ORG company, foundation, advocacy or professional-body page; REPO repository, author page or working-paper series; AGG aggregator or index; ENC encyclopaedia.
5. **Snowballing** was through result lists only. Citation chaining was not run.

## 5. Contradictions and counter-evidence

**5.1 Contradictions and summary self-contradictions**
1. **Pew 2013, "most others would want it":** 65% (Q-18) versus 68% (Q-30). Unresolved; primary text unread; range 65-68% recorded (W7-08).
2. **Q-18's gloss contradicts its own numbers.** It says the gap suggests "people underestimate others' desire for life extension", but 56% personally declining while 65% expect others to want it implies respondents expect others to want it more than they do. Gloss discarded.
3. **Q-22's cross-survey comparison is not like-for-like.** It says recent surveys show higher interest than Pew 2013's 9%; that compares "more than 100 as ideal lifespan" (2013) with "reach 100" (2025) and "aim to reach 100" (OLP). Discarded. Its unattributed line "23% of 18-34s vs 17% of 65+ would live beyond 100" is not used.
4. **Demand depends on framing.** Pew 2013 (56% no; 9% over 100), Barnett 2021 (33% yes, 42% no) and Partridge 2011 (one-third would use a pill) sit against Donner 2016 (42% unlimited lifespan, 20% would die at 85 when health is stipulated). The questions differ, so this is a framing difference, not a factual conflict. Aparicio 2025 (W7-13) says studies stress life extension over healthspan. The high-demand result comes from authors with company roles (W7-10). No source measured behaviour or willingness to pay.
5. **Economic magnitudes are not comparable** (AUTHOR-JUDGMENT). Goldman: $7.1 trillion over 50 years for +2.2 years, US microsimulation. Scott et al.: $38 trillion per +1 year, basis unknown. Preprint: $408 billion per year of US GDP, $27.1 trillion net present value. Murphy and Topel: past gains, welfare "wealth" of about $3.2 trillion per year. They differ in concept (welfare value, GDP, cumulative), horizon and discounting, so the report should not combine or rank them.
6. **Value versus cost inside one modelling programme.** Goldman's results show higher entitlement outlays alongside the $7.1 trillion value (W7-01, W7-02). No source retrieved challenges the valuations themselves (Q-07 returned advocacy and policy items, not critiques).
7. **Attribution problems in summaries.** Q-07's paragraphs on actuarial-model assumptions and dependency ratios could not be tied to one named link. Q-15's attribution of the "Faustian bargain" commentary to Olshansky is unverified. Q-14's interview shares may come from silverlinings.bio rather than the preprint. Q-31 said the young-adult figures typed into the query were not displayed; Q-27 had supplied them and the pooled figures are consistent. Q-29 corrected my premise: the Pew aging survey ran 2-8 September 2025 and was released in November.
8. **Echo and hype.** The $38 trillion figure recurs on nmn.com, fightaging.org, longevity.technology and a Buck Institute blog (Q-02, Q-06). One origin; not counted.

## 9. Limitations

- **Search budget.** The shared session cap ended retrieval at Q-37. This file answers (a) and (c), and only partly (b), (d) and (e). It is not a complete W7.
- **Summary-mediated figures.** Nothing was checked against primary text. Some summaries contained errors or glosses (Section 5.1), and several figures rest on one summary.
- **Origin rule.** Under amendment v2.1 no figure reached F3. That is honest for single-study numbers, but it means every W7 figure is single-confirmation and none is a model input under §8 of the analysis plan.
- **Leading queries.** Q-05, Q-06, Q-08, Q-13, Q-14, Q-18, Q-26, Q-29 and Q-31 carried figures. Their echoes are flagged; 38% (Pew 2013) rests on a leading query alone.
- **Stakes** were taken from result summaries and secondary pages (company biographies, a professional-network page), not from paper disclosure statements.
- **Comparability.** Survey questions differ in wording and threshold. Valuations differ in concept. Neither set should be pooled.
- **US-centric.** 8 of 12 cards with a stated population are US. No low- or middle-income-country evidence was retrieved.
- **Tool messages.** No retrieved page addressed the agent. The search wrapper's trailing reminder to cite sources and its budget-refusal notice were treated as tool messages, and identifiers are given as URLs in each card.
