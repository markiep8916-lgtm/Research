## Abstract

**Background.** Maura Murray, a 21-year-old nursing student, vanished after a single-car crash on Route 112 in Haverhill, New Hampshire, on the evening of February 9, 2004. As of 2026-09-28 the case is open and officially described as a suspicious disappearance. **Purpose.** To determine which family of explanations the public record supports best, and with what confidence. **Method.** Document analysis of 73 tier-graded sources, organised as an Analysis of Competing Hypotheses across six hypothesis families, with reference-class priors from search-and-rescue, forensic and missing-person research and a sensitivity analysis. Retrieval was limited to search-index snippets because the session's network policy blocked full-text access. **Findings.** The record establishes that she left the car alive, functional, and intending to avoid police; that a scent trail ran a short distance east along the road and ended; that extensive but late searches found nothing; that the state has treated the case as a potential homicide since 2007 while still searching terrain in 2022; and that nothing has surfaced in 22 years. Third-party harm after she left on foot, most plausibly by a passing motorist, is judged the most likely explanation (45 to 60%), with death by exposure or misadventure on foot a close second (25 to 35%). Voluntary disappearance, a planned companion, and official involvement are remote. **Implications.** The order of the two live explanations depends on the adequacy of the 2004 search coverage and on the undisclosed basis of the state's crime framing; the report names the observations that would settle it.

**Keywords**: missing persons; cold case; Analysis of Competing Hypotheses; lost-person behaviour; hypothermia; search and rescue; websleuthing; New Hampshire

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Context

On the evening of Monday, February 9, 2004, a black 1996 Saturn sedan left Route 112, also called Wild Ammonoosuc Road, at a sharp bend in Haverhill, New Hampshire. The driver, Maura Murray, a nursing student at the University of Massachusetts Amherst and a former West Point cadet, spoke briefly with a passing motorist, declined his help, and was gone by the time the first police officer arrived a few minutes later. The state's Cold Case Unit describes what followed in one sentence: "She has not been seen or heard from since." (New Hampshire Department of Justice [NH DOJ], n.d.) <!--ref:nhdoj_victimpage--><!--anchor:quote:She%20has%20not%20been%20seen%20or%20heard%20from%20since.-->

Twenty-two years later the case remains open. It has been the subject of a book, a television series, several long-running podcasts, a family-produced podcast, an FBI ViCAP alert, a state supreme court decision on the family's access to records, a consensual FBI-assisted dig in a neighbour's basement, and a 2022 ground search five miles from the crash. It has been called the "first crime mystery of the social media age" (WDTN, n.d.) <!--ref:wdtn_socialmedia--><!--anchor:quote:first%20crime%20mystery%20of%20the%20social%20media%20age-->, and the criminological literature on the amateur investigation it spawned is, by its own description, "somewhat thin on the ground" (Yardley et al., 2018) <!--ref:yardley2018--><!--anchor:quote:Research%20around%20websleuthing%20is%20somewhat%20thin%20on%20the%20ground-->.

### 1.2 Problem

Public discussion of the case is organised into camps, each of which assembles the facts that fit its explanation: that she was abducted and killed by a local; that she walked into the woods and died; that she took her own life; that she staged a disappearance and is alive; that she was travelling with someone; or that the police were involved and covered it up. The facts themselves are unstable. The distance the tracking dog followed her scent is reported five different ways. The position of a box of wine in the car is reported two ways. The time the first officer arrived is reported two ways. Some widely repeated details trace to a single author's reading of documents the public has never seen. The problem this report addresses is not the absence of information but its contamination: which claims the official and contemporaneous record actually establishes, and what those claims imply when each explanation is tested against all of them at once.

### 1.3 Purpose and research question

The report asks: *Given the publicly available evidentiary record as of 2026-09-28, which family of explanations for Maura Murray's disappearance on the evening of February 9, 2004 is most consistent with that record, and how much confidence does the record support?* Three sub-questions structure the findings: (1) what the record establishes about February 5 to 11, 2004 and which elements are disputed; (2) which evidence items discriminate between the six hypothesis families and what the relevant base rates imply; and (3) what the investigation's public statements and actions from 2004 to 2026 reveal about its working hypothesis, and what would resolve the remaining uncertainty.

### 1.4 Significance and boundaries

A living family has asked for accurate, evidence-based attention to the case, and an active investigation is applying new methods to it. A structured comparison of explanations that separates what is established from what is asserted is useful to both. The report does not attempt to identify a perpetrator. It analyses hypothesis families, not people, and it does not name any uncharged private individual. Where a private witness is known online by name, the report refers to the person by role.

## 2. Analytical Framework and Base Rates

This section sets out the reference classes against which the case evidence is read. Each is a partial match for the case at best, and Section 5 returns to the mismatches.

### 2.1 What usually happens to missing adults

Most missing adults are found quickly and alive. The National Institute of Justice's summary of the National Missing and Unidentified Persons System (NamUs) reports that "About 75 percent of missing adults are found within 24 to 72 hours" (National Institute of Justice [NIJ], n.d.-a) <!--ref:nij_lostnotforgotten--><!--anchor:quote:About%2075%20percent%20of%20missing%20adults%20are%20found%20within%2024%20to%2072%20hours.-->, and that among resolved NamUs cases "9,584 (76 percent) were located alive, and 3,037 (24 percent) were found to be deceased" (NIJ, n.d.-a) <!--ref:nij_lostnotforgotten--><!--anchor:quote:9%2C584%20%2876%20percent%29%20were%20located%20alive%2C%20and%203%2C037%20%2824%20percent%29%20were%20found%20to%20be%20deceased-->. The United Kingdom's national data show the same shape: in 2022/23, "88.3% of missing people being found within the first 48 hours in England & Wales" (UK Missing Persons Unit, 2024) <!--ref:ukmpu2023--><!--anchor:quote:88.3%25%20of%20missing%20people%20being%20found%20within%20the%20first%2048%20hours%20in%20England%20%26%20Wales-->. The largest qualitative study of missing adults found that "Most people go missing intentionally, to escape family or other problems, but others may not make a deliberate decision" (Biehal et al., 2003) <!--ref:biehal2003--><!--anchor:quote:Most%20people%20go%20missing%20intentionally%2C%20to%20escape%20family%20or%20other%20problems%2C%20but%20others%20may%20not%20make%20a%20deliberate%20decision.-->.

These figures are dominated by short episodes and are the wrong reference class for a case that has stayed open for 22 years. The relevant conditional is the outcome distribution for cases that remain unresolved for years, and no source retrieved in this run reports it. Two inferences are still safe. First, intentional absence is common but overwhelmingly short-lived, so a 22-year intentional absence with no financial, digital, or family contact lies far into the tail of the intentional group. Second, a registry study of NamUs cases found "the level of violence in the database was lower than expected, at about 10% of all missing persons cases" (NIJ, n.d.-b) <!--ref:nij_violence--><!--anchor:quote:the%20level%20of%20violence%20in%20the%20database%20was%20lower%20than%20expected%2C%20at%20about%2010%25%20of%20all%20missing%20persons%20cases.-->; that figure counts documented violence and necessarily undercounts cases in which no body and no offender were ever found, which is the situation here. The report therefore treats the violence base rate as a floor, not a point estimate.

### 2.2 Where lost and despondent people are found

Search-and-rescue planning rests on the International Search and Rescue Incident Database (ISRID), which by 2024 held "data from more than 145,000 searches from around the world" (Koester, 2008, as described in a 2024 interview) <!--ref:koester2008--><!--anchor:quote:data%20from%20more%20than%20145%2C000%20searches%20from%20around%20the%20world-->. The database's "despondent" profile is the closest match for a distressed young adult who leaves a vehicle at night. In the Virginia data that seeded the profile, "this type of search accounts for 14% of missing person searches reported" (dbS Productions, n.d.) <!--ref:dbs_despondent--><!--anchor:quote:in%20the%20Virginia%20database%20this%20type%20of%20search%20accounts%20for%2014%25%20of%20missing%20person%20searches%20reported-->, and the profile notes that "These subjects often have set out into the woods in order to commit suicide, few are truly lost, but many are in critical condition" (dbS Productions, n.d.) <!--ref:dbs_despondent--><!--anchor:quote:These%20subjects%20often%20have%20set%20out%20into%20the%20woods%20in%20order%20to%20commit%20suicide%2C%20few%20are%20truly%20lost%2C%20but%20many%20are%20in%20critical%20condition-->. The profile's distance quantiles for despondent subjects, which would allow a quantitative statement about how far from the car a body would be expected, could not be retrieved in this session [MATERIAL GAP: despondent-category distance quantiles from the lost-person reference (Koester, 2008) <!--ref:koester2008--><!--anchor:section:Despondent%20profile--> not retrieved]. The point is qualitative only: such subjects are usually found close to the point last seen, and the relevant question for this case is whether the searches covered that near zone adequately.

### 2.3 Hypothermia, alcohol, and concealment

Lethal hypothermia produces behaviour that hides bodies. In a series of "69 cases of death due to lethal hypothermia between 1978 and 1994" (Rothschild & Schneider, 1995) <!--ref:rothschild1995--><!--anchor:quote:69%20cases%20of%20death%20due%20to%20lethal%20hypothermia%20between%201978%20and%201994-->, "paradoxical undressing occurred in 25% of the cases" and "Nearly all bodies with partial or complete disrobement were found in a position which indicated a final mechanism of protection" (Rothschild & Schneider, 1995) <!--ref:rothschild1995--><!--anchor:quote:Nearly%20all%20bodies%20with%20partial%20or%20complete%20disrobement%20were%20found%20in%20a%20position%20which%20indicated%20a%20final%20mechanism%20of%20protection-->, a pattern the authors named terminal burrowing, which "occurred predominantly with slow decreases in temperature and moderately cold conditions" (Rothschild & Schneider, 1995) <!--ref:rothschild1995--><!--anchor:quote:occurred%20predominantly%20with%20slow%20decreases%20in%20temperature%20and%20moderately%20cold%20conditions-->. Alcohol raises the risk: a forensic series reported that "a blood alcohol concentration of over 2.5 mg/ml had often been found in people in their forties and fifties who had died from hypothermia" (Alcohol and Alcoholism, 1984) <!--ref:alcalc1984--><!--anchor:quote:a%20blood%20alcohol%20concentration%20of%20over%202.5%20mg%2Fml%20had%20often%20been%20found%20in%20people%20in%20their%20forties%20and%20fifties%20who%20had%20died%20from%20hypothermia-->, and controlled cold-exposure work shows alcohol impairs thermoregulation (Freund et al., 1994) <!--ref:freund1994--><!--anchor:section:Abstract-->. For this case the implication is narrow but important: a person who dies of exposure after drinking may crawl into cover, which lowers the detection probability of both ground and aerial searches, so the absence of a body after a search is weaker evidence against exposure than intuition suggests.

### 2.4 How much a scent trail can tell

The only controlled study of bloodhound trailing under realistic conditions ran trails in which "after 48 hours the dogs were cued with scent collected from various parts of the trail-setter's body and required to follow the trail" (Harvey & Harvey, 2003) <!--ref:harvey2003--><!--anchor:quote:after%2048%20hours%20the%20dogs%20were%20cued%20with%20scent%20collected%20from%20various%20parts%20of%20the%20trail-setter%27s%20body%20and%20required%20to%20follow%20the%20trail-->, and concluded that "a veteran bloodhound can trail and correctly identify a person under various conditions" and that "the potential error rate of a veteran bloodhound-handler team is low" (Harvey & Harvey, 2003) <!--ref:harvey2003--><!--anchor:quote:the%20potential%20error%20rate%20of%20a%20veteran%20bloodhound-handler%20team%20is%20low-->. Two cautions apply when that result is carried to Route 112. The study's trails had known, uncontaminated start points; a crash scene visited by a passer-by, police, a tow operator and passing traffic before the dog arrived does not. And a trail that ends on a road surface is consistent with a vehicle pickup, with scent loss on a surface disturbed by tyres and salt, and with the subject having stepped off the road at a point the dog did not resolve. The end of a trail is evidence about where the trail became unworkable, which is not the same as evidence about where the person went.

### 2.5 The analytic method

The hypothesis comparison follows the Analysis of Competing Hypotheses (ACH), whose central instruction is that "Some evidence will have greater 'diagnosticity' than other evidence" (Heuer, 1999) <!--ref:heuer1999--><!--anchor:quote:Some%20evidence%20will%20have%20greater%20%27diagnosticity%27%20than%20other%20evidence-->: a hypothesis is ranked by how little evidence contradicts it, not by how much appears to support it, and evidence consistent with every hypothesis is set aside. The method has been evaluated experimentally with mixed results on bias reduction (Dhami et al., 2019) <!--ref:dhami2019--><!--anchor:section:Abstract-->, which is why this report exposes the full matrix rather than asking the reader to trust a ranking. ACH has a known weakness that matters here: a vague hypothesis can score well simply because it is consistent with everything. Section 4.3 applies a correction for it.

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Design

A pragmatist, abductive document analysis. Six hypothesis families were fixed in the Phase 1 Methodology Blueprint before evidence collection began (Appendix C lists the phase artifacts). Evidence was then collected in four parallel sweeps (the official record and timeline; physical evidence and searches; competing explanations and their proponents; developments from 2019 to 2026) and one framework sweep for base-rate literature.

### 3.2 Sources and tiers

Seventy-three sources entered the corpus. Each was assigned a tier that governs what it may support:

| Tier | Source class | May support |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Official: NH DOJ Cold Case Unit and press releases; FBI ViCAP; the 2006 state supreme court opinion | Any fact, alone |
| 2 | Contemporaneous and established journalism (Boston Globe 2004; AP wire summaries of police records; Concord Monitor; Union Leader; NHPR; CBS, NBC and regional outlets) | Any fact, alone |
| 3 | Later long-form and network features (A&E; Oxygen companion articles; E! Online) | Facts with corroboration; positions alone |
| 4 | Participants (the Murray family site and podcast; officials' words quoted there) | Their own positions; documentary reproductions |
| 5 | Non-participant books and podcasts (the 2016 book on the case; the Missing Maura Murray podcast) | Their own theses |
| 6 | Peer-reviewed and institutional base-rate literature | Reference classes |
| 7 | Amateur and independent-research sites, forums, tertiary summaries | Detail and context only; never the sole support for a factual claim |

A fact is called established when a tier 1 source states it or two independent tier 2 sources state it; otherwise it is labelled single-source or reconstruction. The Source Verification Report grades every source on the suite's two-axis rubric.

### 3.3 Analytic steps

(1) Reconstruct the timeline of February 5 to 11, 2004 with a source attached to every time and every physical fact. (2) Inventory evidence items and grade their best source. (3) Build the ACH matrix, coding each item against each hypothesis as strongly consistent (CC), consistent (C), neutral (N), inconsistent (I), or strongly inconsistent (II). (4) Discard non-diagnostic rows. (5) State a prior for each family from the base-rate literature, in words and as a range. (6) Rank by weighted inconsistency, then adjust by the priors, applying a positive-evidence requirement to any hypothesis whose low inconsistency could reflect vagueness. (7) Run a sensitivity analysis on the most contested items and on the priors. (8) State the conclusion with ranges, the judgments it depends on, and the observations that would change it. Devil's Advocate checkpoints were run after Phases 1, 3 and 5.

### 3.4 Validity measures

Source triangulation and tier discipline; an earliest-attestation rule for load-bearing facts; a contradictions table rather than silent resolution; every citation carries a hidden reference key and a locator (a verbatim quotation or a section) so it can be checked against `phase2_investigation/corpus.yaml`; every "current" statement is anchored to 2026-09-28.

### 3.5 Retrieval constraints

Two constraints shaped the evidence and are repeated in the Limitations. The session's network policy returned an egress-blocked error for every full-text fetch attempted, including state and federal government sites, news sites, court-opinion repositories, the Internet Archive and Wikipedia; all evidence therefore comes from search-index snippets attributed to URLs. The session's search budget (200 queries) was exhausted before roughly 70 planned follow-up queries could run. Quotations in this report are verbatim snippet strings; a reader relying on any single quotation should check it at the cited URL.

### 3.6 Ethics

Document analysis only; no human subjects. No uncharged private individual is named or characterised as a suspect. Facts about the victim's private life appear only where a reputable outlet published them and they bear on a hypothesis. AI assistance is disclosed above.

### 4.3 The hypotheses and the evidence matrix (sub-question 2)

**Hypotheses.** H1: left on foot; died of exposure or misadventure (a fall, the river); body not found. H2: left on foot intending to die; body not found. H3: left on foot alive; killed or fatally harmed by a third party, in three sub-variants: H3a, accepted or was forced into a passing vehicle; H3b, went to a nearby residence; H3c, was followed or intercepted by someone connected to her. H4: left deliberately to start a new life. H5: was travelling with, or met, a planned companion who removed her from the scene. H6: was removed by, or with the knowledge of, a responding official, with a cover-up.

**Matrix.** Codes: CC strongly consistent, C consistent, N neutral, I inconsistent, II strongly inconsistent. The best source's grade is given in parentheses.

**Correction for vagueness.** H3 has the lowest tally partly because "an unknown person did something unknown somewhere unknown" is consistent with almost any pattern. The correction applied is a positive-evidence requirement: H3 is ranked first only because three items point toward it rather than merely failing to contradict it, namely the track ending on the road with the supervisor's own note about a vehicle (E10), the state's crime framing and ViCAP request (E15), and the absence of remains in terrain that has been searched, hunted and lived in for 22 years (E19). Each of those is also readable under H1, which is why the two are close.

**Priors.** For a distressed, drinking young adult who leaves a disabled car on a rural winter road at night and is not found within days, the reference classes in Section 2 support these judgmental prior ranges before the case-specific evidence: death on foot (H1 plus H2) 45 to 60%; third-party harm 20 to 35%; a companion 5 to 10%; voluntary permanent disappearance 5 to 10%; official involvement 1 to 3%. Conditioning on "still unresolved after 22 years" moves weight from the on-foot family toward third-party harm, because on-foot deaths near roads are usually resolved by a chance find within years, while concealed homicides are not.

| 2 | H1, exposure or misadventure on foot | 25 to 35% | Strong positive support from E07, E08 and E12; inconsistent only with the search negatives, mitigated by thermal-imaging timing, terminal burrowing and the Largay precedent |
| 3 | H2, suicide on foot | 5 to 10% | Shares H1's search problem; weakened by the lodging enquiries; indistinguishable from H1 by outcome |
| 4 | H5, planned companion | 2 to 5% | No positive evidence of a companion survives; strongly inconsistent with the eastbound report if that was her |
| 5 | H4, new life | 1 to 3% | Strong pre-event support, but 22 years without a trace and the state's posture are close to fatal |
| 6 | H6, official involvement | 1 to 3% | One contested account; contradicted by her own police avoidance, the eastbound report, and the state's conduct (FBI participation, ViCAP) |

The ranges are judgmental, sum to roughly 100% at their midpoints, and are conditional on the public record.

**Sensitivity.** Treating the dog track as uninformative narrows the gap between H3 and H1 without reordering them. Treating the eastbound report as not her removes H1's strongest item and helps H3. Treating the search negatives as weak, because the 2004 coverage was thinner than described or because she reached the Wild Ammonoosuc River, brings H1 to parity with H3. Holding the violence prior at its 10% floor rather than raising it for a 22-year-unresolved case puts H1 first. Accepting the February 11 voicemail as her collapses H1 and H2 and raises H3 (captivity) and H4, with H3 still first. Accepting the early-police-vehicle account raises H6 to the 10 to 20% range, with H3 still first. The ranking of H3 over H1 is therefore robust to any single evidence item but not to two structural judgments: how much a 22-year absence of remains says about a death on foot, and how much the state's conduct reveals about evidence it holds.

### 4.4 The investigation's revealed working hypothesis (sub-question 3)

The state has said three things consistently since 2007: that it is a criminal investigation and potential homicide; that no one has been ruled out; and that it does not know what happened. Its actions say more than its words. Requesting a ViCAP entry in 2022 means the office wanted the case compared against violent crimes nationally, which is an act that presupposes a possible offender. Searching woods five miles east in the same year, on the strength of a 2004 report of someone on foot, means the office had not excluded an on-foot journey east. Digging a neighbour's basement with the FBI in 2019 at the family's urging, without probable cause and with consent, means the office was willing to test a foul-play lead it did not itself credit. Applying "new technology" to "existing evidence" in 2026 means it holds physical evidence it considers worth re-examining. Taken together, the investigators' revealed working hypothesis is the same pair this report arrives at: violent crime by an unknown person, or death on foot in terrain, with the office declining to choose in public.

What would resolve it, in descending order of leverage: (1) the basis for the 2007 "potential homicide" framing and the 2022 ViCAP request, which the family litigated for and the state has kept sealed; (2) a search-coverage record for 2004 and 2022, including whether and how the river was searched, and the 2022 outcome; (3) whether the person seen moving east at 8:00 to 8:30 PM was ever confirmed or excluded; (4) phone forensics on the February 11 voicemail and on her handset's last network contact; (5) what the 2026 technology work is applied to, terrain or evidence. Items 2, 3 and 5 would move the ranking between H3 and H1; item 1 could settle it.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Interpretation

The report's central finding is that only two explanations survive contact with the whole record, and that they share a first act. On every hypothesis that the evidence supports, Maura Murray left the car alive, on foot, functional enough to gather her wallet, phone and the alcohol she had bought, and unwilling to wait for the police she knew were coming. That first act is not a matter of interpretation: the state's own page records the conversation with the passing motorist, the FBI's alert records her false statement that she had called for help, and the dispatch log records that she was gone within minutes. Her reasons are inference, but the inference is short. She had crashed her father's car two nights earlier, the car she had just crashed smelled of alcohol, and she had told her professors a lie to get away. Whatever she intended for the days ahead, she did not intend to explain herself to a police officer that night.

Where the two explanations part is what happened on the road. On the exposure reading, a shivering, hatless, intoxicated young woman walking east on a dark February road in the White Mountains foothills is a search-and-rescue emergency, and the literature says such subjects seldom go far, often seek shelter, and can end in cover that ground teams and dogs miss. On the third-party reading, the same woman on the same road is a target of opportunity for anyone who stops, which is the family's view and that of a retired state police lieutenant who worked the case: "She got into the wrong car. She went to the wrong house." (Oxygen, n.d.) <!--ref:oxygen_keytheories--><!--anchor:quote:She%20got%20into%20the%20wrong%20car.%20She%20went%20to%20the%20wrong%20house.--> The report ranks the second reading first because three facts lean its way, but it would be a misreading of the ranges to call the matter settled. The most consequential single fact for the exposure reading is one the online debate underweights: the thermal-imaging helicopter flew roughly 36 hours after the crash, and a person who died on the first night would have had no heat signature by then. The most consequential single fact for the third-party reading is one the debate overweights: a scent trail lost on a plowed, trafficked road after 40 hours is, by the search supervisor's own relayed remark, weak evidence of a vehicle.

The four remote explanations fail for different reasons. Voluntary permanent disappearance has the best pre-event evidence of any hypothesis and the worst post-event evidence: 22 years without a card used, a phone switched on, a sighting judged credible, or a family contact, in a case with national publicity and an FBI alert, is close to disqualifying, and the author who advanced the thesis did so, by a tertiary summary, on the basis that she might "take on a new identity due to fears her pending credit card fraud case would prevent her being hired as a nurse" (Wikipedia, n.d.-a) <!--ref:wikipedia_tca--><!--anchor:quote:take%20on%20a%20new%20identity%20due%20to%20fears%20her%20pending%20credit%20card%20fraud%20case%20would%20prevent%20her%20being%20hired%20as%20a%20nurse-->, a motive that does not survive two decades of silence. The planned-companion explanation has no surviving positive evidence once the first caller's "man smoking" report is read with her later revision, and it is hard to square with the report of someone on foot five miles east an hour later. The official-involvement explanation rests on one motorist's account relayed by amateur sites and contradicted by the dispatch-log reproductions; it also requires the same office that later brought in the FBI to dig a basement and requested a ViCAP entry to have been hiding the answer all along. Suicide on foot is a real possibility, but it inherits every problem of the exposure reading and adds one: a person who has resolved to die does not usually spend the afternoon telephoning about a condominium and a tourist office.

### 5.2 Connection to the literature

The base rates behaved as expected in shape but were too coarse in level. Population-wide missing-person outcomes (NIJ, n.d.-a) <!--ref:nij_lostnotforgotten--><!--anchor:section:Body--> (UK Missing Persons Unit, 2024) <!--ref:ukmpu2023--><!--anchor:section:Body--> say almost nothing about a 22-year case; the violence rate in NamUs (NIJ, n.d.-b) <!--ref:nij_violence--><!--anchor:section:Body--> is a floor; the despondent-profile literature (dbS Productions, n.d.) <!--ref:dbs_despondent--><!--anchor:section:Body--> supplies the qualitative expectation of a short journey and a critical condition; the hypothermia series (Rothschild & Schneider, 1995) <!--ref:rothschild1995--><!--anchor:section:Abstract--> supplies the mechanism by which a body evades discovery; the bloodhound study (Harvey & Harvey, 2003) <!--ref:harvey2003--><!--anchor:section:Abstract--> supplies confidence in the dog and, by its design, the reason to discount a trail that ends on asphalt. Two analogues bracket the exposure reading. A Maine hiker's remains lay "just two miles from the Appalachian Trail" (NBC News, 2016) <!--ref:largay_nbc--><!--anchor:quote:Largay%2C%20who%20died%20while%20waiting%20for%20help%20that%20never%20arrived%2C%20was%20just%20two%20miles%20from%20the%20Appalachian%20Trail.--> and were found "more than two years after she went missing, in October 2015" by "a contractor conducting a forestry survey" (CBS Boston, n.d.) <!--ref:largay_cbs--><!--anchor:quote:more%20than%20two%20years%20after%20she%20went%20missing%2C%20in%20October%202015%2C%20that%20her%20remains%20were%20found%20by%20a%20contractor%20conducting%20a%20forestry%20survey-->, after a large search. That case shows a body can be missed; it also shows it is usually found, and 22 years is an order of magnitude longer.

The report also confirms, at the level of one case, what the websleuthing literature describes at the level of the field (Yardley et al., 2018) <!--ref:yardley2018--><!--anchor:section:Abstract-->: the podcast that became the case's main amplifier began "focusing on the armchair detectives who became increasingly obsessed with Maura Murray's disappearance" (CrimeCon, n.d.) <!--ref:crimecon_mmm--><!--anchor:quote:focusing%20on%20the%20armchair%20detectives%20who%20became%20increasingly%20obsessed%20with%20Maura%20Murray%27s%20disappearance--> and "was the inspiration for Oxygen Network's The Disappearance of Maura Murray TV show" (CrimeCon, n.d.) <!--ref:crimecon_mmm--><!--anchor:quote:Missing%20Maura%20Murray%20was%20the%20inspiration%20for%20Oxygen%20Network%27s%20The%20Disappearance%20of%20Maura%20Murray%20TV%20show-->, which the Attorney General's office in turn said contained inaccuracies. The record's instability is a product of that ecosystem, and tier discipline is the only defence a document analysis has against it.

### 5.3 Implications

For public discussion: the honest statement of the case is not "abducted" or "died in the woods" but "left on foot, then one of two things", and the evidence that would separate the two is mostly held by the state or obtainable by a search-coverage review, not by re-reading the same online timelines. For the investigation, if the report's reading of its revealed hypothesis is right, the highest-leverage public step short of releasing the file is to say whether the 2004 eastbound report has been resolved and whether the river corridor east of the crash was ever searched to modern standards; LiDAR, if that is what the 2026 statement refers to, is exactly the tool for the second question. For the family, the analysis supports the claim it has made since 2004 that this was a temporary trip and that she meant to return, and it supports the "foul play" conclusion as the single most likely outcome, while declining to endorse the "local" qualifier, for which the public record offers no specific support.

### 5.4 Limitations

First and most important, no source was read in full. Every quotation is a verbatim string from a search-index snippet attributed to a URL; attribution is the search tool's, and for several quotations the sweep reports rate it probable rather than confirmed. The report's `corpus.yaml` records every quotation so each can be checked. Second, the search budget ran out mid-investigation; roughly 70 planned follow-up queries, listed in the sweep reports, did not run, including queries for a meteorological record of the night, for the 2022 search outcome, and for contemporaneous 2004 coverage in the regional papers. Third, the investigative file is sealed; the conclusion is conditional on the public record and Section 4.4 says what the file could change. Fourth, the base-rate populations only partly resemble the case, and the despondent-profile distance quantiles were not retrieved. Fifth, the probability ranges are the analyst's judgment, not a computed posterior; the matrix is exposed so a reader can re-weight it. Sixth, H1 and H2 cannot be separated by outcome evidence, and the split between them is the least reliable number in the ranking. Seventh, ACH's known weakness with vague hypotheses was corrected by a positive-evidence requirement that is itself a judgment.

### 5.5 Future work

An unrestricted environment should (a) retrieve the full text of the NH DOJ releases, the FBI alert, the 2006 opinion, the 2004 Boston Globe and regional coverage, and the family site's 911 transcript, and re-run the attribution checks; (b) obtain the February 9, 2004 observations from the nearest cooperative weather station and estimate survival time on foot for a lightly dressed, intoxicated adult; (c) obtain the despondent-profile quantiles from the lost-person reference (Koester, 2008) <!--ref:koester2008--><!--anchor:section:Despondent%20profile--> and map them onto Route 112 east of the crash; (d) reconstruct the 2004 and 2022 search polygons from public reporting and identify the unsearched corridor, including the river; and (e) revisit the matrix if the state discloses the basis of its crime framing.

## 6. Conclusion

On the public record as of 2026-09-28, the most likely explanation for Maura Murray's disappearance is that she left the crash scene alive and on foot, most probably heading east along Route 112 to avoid the police she knew were coming, and was then killed or fatally harmed by someone she encountered on that road, most plausibly a motorist who offered a ride. "East" is the best-supported direction, not an established fact: it rests on the direction the dog took and on the one reported sighting. That explanation is judged to be in the 45 to 60% range. The second explanation, that she died of exposure or misadventure on foot and her body has not been found, is judged to be in the 25 to 35% range and cannot be excluded; it moves to first place if the 2004 search coverage was weaker than described or if stranger violence is held at its population floor. The gap between these two explanations is within the analyst's judgmental error; what separates them is a reading of two facts the public cannot check. Suicide on foot is a distinct third. Voluntary permanent disappearance, a planned companion, and official involvement are remote on this record.

Three recommendations follow. The investigation's most useful public step, short of disclosure, is to state whether the 2004 eastbound report has been resolved and whether the river corridor was searched. Public discussion should retire the details this report found unstable (the track length, the wine box, the "ping", the early police vehicle) and organise itself around the two live explanations. And any future analysis should begin, as this one did, by grading its sources, because in this case the record's contamination is itself one of the findings.

She was 21, a nursing student, shaken and cold at the side of a dark road, and she did not want to be in trouble. Everything the record establishes follows from that; nothing the record establishes yet says what came next.
