## 0. Reading rules

- Every conclusion is tied to a comparator ("replacing SFA with what?"), an outcome type (biomarker vs events vs deaths) and a certainty tier.
- Unsearched domains are NOT SEARCHED, never "no evidence".
- Guideline text is reported as what bodies say, not as evidence.

## 2. Integrated themes

**T1 — "It depends on what replaces it" is the central finding.** Trials and cohorts agree on one structure: swapping SFA for PUFA (and, less so, MUFA or whole grains) is where benefit appears; swapping for refined carbohydrate or leaving the replacement unspecified shows little. The pooled null cohort results (C4) and the trial results with PUFA replacement (C2, C3) are therefore not necessarily contradictory: they answer different questions. The Cochrane/Annals RCTs mix replacement nutrients; only the Annals review reports a PUFA-specific figure.

**T2 — LDL-C is the best-supported mechanism; clinical-event evidence is weaker.** Biomarker evidence is consistent (C1). Events: moderate for the composite, low or very low for individual events and deaths. Reviews agree on this and disagree on framing (Annals authors vs editorial; JMAJ conclusion "cannot be recommended at present").

**T3 — Risk stratification reconciles much of the trial disagreement.** The Annals review finds the benefit concentrated in high-risk people. JMAJ pooled fewer, mostly secondary-prevention trials (9 trials, 13,532 participants, search to April 2023) and found no significant differences. The displayed text does not say which trials JMAJ excluded; an explanation based on trial selection is plausible but unverified.

**T4 — Foods are not nutrients.** Cheese vs butter, dairy vs meat and coconut vs olive oil results show that SFA content alone does not predict the response. Comparators must not be merged: cheese lowers LDL-C versus butter by 3.3% but is 5.3% higher than a MUFA diet and 12.3% higher than a PUFA diet (Brassard) [S5-12].

**T5 — The natural/industrial distinction is real for trans fat and largely orthogonal for saturated fat.** Trans fat is unsaturated. Industrial trans fat has the strongest evidence of harm. Natural ruminant trans fat is not lipid-neutral per gram. For SFA itself the retrieved evidence does not support a "natural SFA is safer" or "industrial SFA is worse" claim; butter, tallow and coconut oil are natural yet raise LDL-C relative to unsaturated oils, while "seed oils" (industrial) are associated with lower mortality in US cohorts and are rated as having evidence of benefit by a 2026 clinician's guide (V1) [S5-17, S6-20].

**T6 — Policy and science have decoupled in the US in 2026.** The DGA keeps the numeric cap while foregrounding SFA-rich foods; AHA, ACC and Harvard call this inconsistent; HHS describes it as ending a "war on saturated fats". No quantitative modelling of the 10% cap against the recommended foods was seen on either side [S1-B5].

**T7 — Funding and framing shape the debate.** Industry-funded evidence exists on the favourable side for dairy and ruminant trans fat (National Dairy Council-funded 2026 meta-analysis; authors of the Astrup 2020 critique declare dairy, beef and palm-oil ties), and the sugar-industry history is documented (Kearns 2016). Symmetric conflict-of-interest data for the defending documents were NOT retrieved, so the record is one-sided by search coverage.

## 4. Gaps (NOT SEARCHED vs evidence-poor)

NOT SEARCHED in this run: chemistry and food composition of SFA; intake data; WHO 2023 SFA guideline, EFSA, SACN, Nordic, Canadian, Australian, Japanese, Chinese guidance; foundational lipid meta-analyses (Mensink 2003/2016, Clarke 1997); LDL/apoB causality (Mendelian randomization, EAS consensus); landmark diet-heart trials and reanalyses (Sydney, Minnesota, LA VA, Finnish, Oslo, MRC, DART, WHI, PREDIMED, Lyon); biomarker pooled studies (FORCE, EPIC-InterAct); liver fat, adiposity, cognition, inflammation and microbiota; susceptibility (familial hypercholesterolaemia, hyper-responders, APOE, children, older adults); seed-oil primary literature; palm oil, MCT, cocoa butter/stearic acid, individual fatty acids; plant vs animal SFA; ultra-processed food; molecular identity of natural vs synthetic fat; hydrogenation; interesterification; fat substitutes; refined-oil contaminants; limits of nutritional epidemiology.

Evidence-poor even where searched: hard-outcome trials by food source (none found); head-to-head natural vs industrial SFA (none found); symmetric conflict-of-interest accounting.

## 5. Source-quality cautions

- Machine-written summaries blend pages, echo numbers placed in queries (S3, S1) and misattribute figures (S2: 15,509 participants; RR 0.86; S8: 12 trials/53,758 attached to the Annals paper).
- Advocacy and trade sources (CSPI, PCRM, dairy and coconut industry pages) were used only for discovery or labelled SECONDARY.
- Funding and conflict-of-interest statements were not displayed for most sources.
- A late-2025 *Annals* review is the first place to read for the current trial-level picture; its accompanying editorial and the authors' reported objection show the interpretation is contested.
