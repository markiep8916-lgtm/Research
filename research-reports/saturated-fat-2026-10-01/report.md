## Bottom line

What replaces saturated fat, and the food it comes in, matter more than the fat alone. Swapping butter or coconut oil for unsaturated plant oils lowers low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and trials suggest a modest reduction in cardiovascular events with no clear effect on deaths. Among "artificial" fats, only industrial trans fat, which is an unsaturated fat, was clearly shown to be harmful; most other artificial fats were not searched.

## Key takeaways

*Certainty labels are the report author's judgments under the rule in Section 2 (high, moderate, low, or not assessed), not the sources' own ratings.*

1. **What replaces the fat matters.** Cohort studies that model a specific swap find lower heart-disease risk when polyunsaturated fat replaces saturated fat, while pooled cohorts that leave the replacement unspecified find little. One trial review reports a benefit specific to polyunsaturated fat, but Cochrane-derived texts disagree on which replacement works best. Certainty: low.
2. **Cholesterol.** In controlled trials butter raised LDL cholesterol relative to olive oil, and coconut oil raised it relative to non-tropical vegetable oils; the American Heart Association (AHA), the American College of Cardiology (ACC) and European guidelines describe the same effect. Certainty: high for butter and coconut oil, for blood measures only. No retrieved trial tested tallow or palm oil, and the foundational feeding-trial meta-analyses were not searched.
3. **Heart-disease events.** Cochrane (2020) pooled 12 trials with mixed replacement nutrients and found about 17% fewer combined cardiovascular events when saturated fat was reduced (a relative risk (RR) of 0.83; 95% confidence interval 0.70 to 0.98), roughly one event avoided per 56 people in primary prevention, and moderate-certainty evidence of little or no effect on deaths (all-cause RR 0.96; 0.90 to 1.03). A December 2025 *Annals of Internal Medicine* review reports the benefit is concentrated in high-risk people; a smaller 2025 review found no significant differences. Certainty: moderate for the combined-events result and for a small-or-no effect on deaths; low that the benefit is concentrated in higher-risk people.
4. **The food matters.** In trials, cheese raised LDL cholesterol less than butter but more than unsaturated fat. In cohorts, dairy overall is neutral to inversely associated with heart disease, with exceptions; butter went with somewhat higher total mortality than plant oils, though not cardiovascular mortality; processed meat is consistently associated with harm, although the randomized-trial review of red meat rated its certainty low to very low. No retrieved trial measured heart attacks or deaths by food source. Certainty: low to moderate for blood-cholesterol differences, low for outcomes.
5. **Natural versus artificial.** Industrial trans fat is harmful and is not a saturated fat (certainty: high for its effect on blood lipids, moderate for clinical outcomes). Natural (ruminant) trans fat raised LDL cholesterol per gram much as the industrial kind did in feeding trials, while cohort studies show no significant association with wide intervals; the review most favorable to it was funded by the US National Dairy Council. Whether the origin of a saturated fatty acid matters, and the health effects of interesterified, fully hydrogenated, substitute and lab-made fats, were **NOT SEARCHED**. Certainty: low for ruminant trans fat; not assessed for the rest.
6. **Official guidance diverges** (a description, not evidence). The 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans (DGA) keep the cap of 10% of calories from saturated fat but name butter and beef tallow among cooking options; the AHA's January 2026 response said the food advice could lead people past recommended limits, and its March 2026 statement advises unsaturated fat in place of saturated fat. These are wordings reported by secondary summaries; the documents were not read.
7. **Other outcomes and gaps.** For type 2 diabetes, cohorts show no harmful association with total saturated fat (low certainty); an umbrella review reports links with several cancers (certainty unknown). Liver fat, dementia, inflammation, children, older adults, genetic susceptibility, "seed oil" literature, palm oil and individual fatty acids were **NOT SEARCHED**.

## Abstract

**Background:** Dietary guidance on saturated fat is contested, and popular claims contrast "natural" fats with "artificial" or industrial ones. **Purpose:** To summarize the best available evidence on the health effects of saturated fat and on whether natural and artificial fats differ. **Method:** A search-limited narrative evidence review, not a systematic review, run as eight parallel search streams with adversarial, editorial and ethics review. Searching was limited to 200 shared web searches and full texts could not be fetched, so findings come from search summaries labeled by how well they were seen. **Findings:** Reducing or modifying saturated fat, with replacement nutrients mixed, modestly reduced combined cardiovascular events in randomized trials (relative risk 0.83, 95% confidence interval 0.70 to 0.98), with moderate-certainty evidence of little or no effect on deaths; a benefit specific to polyunsaturated fat was reported by one review. Cohort studies agree only when the replacement nutrient is specified. Butter and coconut oil raised low-density lipoprotein cholesterol relative to unsaturated oils; cheese raised it less than butter but more than unsaturated fat. Industrial trans fat, an unsaturated fat, is clearly harmful, and natural trans fat raised cholesterol similarly per gram in feeding trials. Whether a saturated fatty acid's origin matters, and the effects of interesterified fats, fat substitutes, lab-grown fats and most non-cardiovascular outcomes, were not searched. **Implications:** Guidance should name the replacement and the food, and the numbers need re-verification against full texts. **Keywords:** saturated fat; trans fat; cardiovascular disease; dietary guidelines; food matrix; evidence review.

## 1. Introduction and scope

The health effects of saturated fat have been argued for decades, and the argument changed shape in 2026: in January the US dietary guidelines kept a numeric limit on saturated fat while naming butter and beef tallow among cooking options, and in March the AHA advised unsaturated fat in place of saturated fat (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services & U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2026; Lichtenstein et al., 2026). The question asked was: *what does the best available evidence show about the health effects of dietary saturated fat, and do those effects depend on whether the fat is "natural" or "artificial"?*

## 2. Method

**Design.** A search-limited narrative evidence review. It borrows the structure of a systematic review but is not one: there is no registered protocol, no database-level search and no study-level risk-of-bias appraisal. Eight search streams ran in parallel: guidelines and policy; blood lipids and randomized trials; cohort studies; non-cardiovascular outcomes; food sources and individual fatty acids; natural versus artificial fats; controversy and susceptibility; and a 2025–2026 recency sweep. The scoping brief and sub-questions are in `run-notes/rq_brief_and_blueprint.md`.

**Search channel and its limits.** Only the web-search tool worked. The session's allowance of 200 searches was shared by all agents; the eight streams used 196 (30, 17, 30, 11, 31, 32, 16 and 29, in the order listed above) and the dispatcher used four. Every stream was cut off before it finished. Fetching was blocked by the network policy for every scholarly or reference host the dispatcher tested, including PubMed, PubMed Central, Europe PMC, doi.org, major journal sites, WHO, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the US dietary guidelines site and Wikipedia, and for four bibliographic-database interfaces (`run-notes/AGENT_COMMON_INSTRUCTIONS.md` lists most of the web hosts); the search agents' own attempts to fetch the DGA PDF and the JMA Journal page were also blocked. No primary document was opened.

**Evidence labels.** V2 and V1 are defined in the box above. *V0*: the agent knew it from memory without seeing it; V0 items were never used as findings. Search summaries are machine-written, can blend several pages and sometimes repeat numbers that were placed in the query; a number that came back only after being typed into a query was not counted as confirmation. The labels measure transcription fidelity, not independent replication.

**Certainty rule.** *High*: several concordant controlled trials or meta-analyses retrieved, with a consistent direction and the key numbers seen in two or more outputs. *Moderate*: a meta-analysis or large trial whose own authors rate certainty moderate, or concordant evidence whose numbers were seen once. *Low*: observational data, a single or small trial, the authors' own "low" or "very low" rating, or conflicting results. *Not assessed*: not searched. These are the report author's judgments. Sources' own ratings, including those under the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system, are quoted separately and attributed to them. Each table states the study design; no independent quality appraisal was made.

**Review process.** A Devil's Advocate (the adversarial reviewer role in the deep-research skill) examined the scoping brief in parallel with the searches, though its own fact-checking searches were refused because the allowance was spent; a second audited the synthesis. The first draft then received an independent claim audit against the evidence ledgers (the per-stream notes in `evidence-ledgers/`), an editorial review, an ethics review and a third Devil's Advocate scan, and the revised draft was re-audited by a fresh reviewer. The reports are in `run-notes/`, and Appendix C logs what changed.

**Handling of gaps.** Unsearched domains are reported as **NOT SEARCHED**. Where a section would need information the evidence base does not contain, the text carries `[MATERIAL GAP]` instead of filling from memory.

**Cochrane.** Reducing saturated fat for at least two years (an average of about 4.7 years in one summary, V1) lowered combined cardiovascular events by 17% in the 2020 abstract (RR 0.83; V2; Hooper et al., 2020), with 8% of participants having an event. The number needed to treat was 56 in primary prevention (V2): over the trials' follow-up, about 56 people reduced saturated fat for one to avoid an event. A secondary-prevention figure appears as 53 in one summary and 32 in another (V1 each), so the figures do not show a clear gradient by baseline risk. The 2015 version had the same point estimate (interval 0.72 to 0.96; Hooper et al., 2015; V2). A New Zealand Cochrane summary document (text V2) and a 2024 umbrella-review snippet (V1) give a different result (RR 0.79; 0.66 to 0.93; 11 trials) that matches neither abstract; its version could not be resolved and it is not used. Cochrane's meta-regression suggested that greater reductions in saturated fat went with greater reductions in events (V1), and one output says no benefit was seen when the individual components of the composite were analyzed (V1); the definition of the composite was not retrieved. Cochrane rated the death estimates moderate certainty. For all-cause deaths the confidence interval runs from roughly a 10% lower to a 3% higher risk, so a small benefit is not excluded.

**The 2025 *Annals* review.** It stratified trials by baseline cardiovascular risk. As reported, absolute reductions for people at low risk fell below the authors' thresholds of importance (5 and 10 per 1,000 people over five years for fatal and non-fatal outcomes), while those for people at high risk exceeded them (V1 for the thresholds; the low stratum is called "low" in one output and "low to intermediate" in another). Most displayed intervals include 1.0. Funding was listed as "None" (V1); the authors' interests were not seen.

**A dispute over interpretation.** The accompanying editorial argued for a "nuanced" message; a news summary reports that it read the findings as showing saturated fats are unlikely to be harmful for the general population, contrary to the reviewers' own conclusions (V1; editorial not read, attribution inferred; Estruch & Lamuela-Raventós, 2026). On the Science Media Centre's reaction page, as relayed by search summaries (V1; page not read), Professor Nita Forouhi of the University of Cambridge was reported as saying the authors had made some methodological improvements but "introduced new methodological challenges that limit interpretation," and that lower intake is beneficial "not just for those at high risk but for the whole population."

**The *JMA Journal* review.** It pooled about a quarter as many participants as Cochrane (13,532 versus 53,758), searched only to April 2023, and concluded that reducing saturated fat "cannot be recommended at present" (V2); its authors call for trials under best medical care including statins. Seven of its nine trials were secondary-prevention trials (people with existing disease), the highest-risk group, yet it found no significant differences, so the *Annals* review's risk-stratification explanation is not established. Its null result on deaths is compatible with the Cochrane and *Annals* estimates, whose intervals also include 1.0.

**Two further meta-analyses.** Harcombe et al. (2015) and the later 10-trial analysis (Table 4) both test interventions that mix replacement nutrients, as Cochrane's pooled composite does (Harcombe: size V2, result V1; later analysis V1).

Three patterns stand out. First, pooled analyses with an unspecified replacement are consistently null, and de Souza and colleagues rated the evidence "very low" certainty; the Chowdhury analysis was corrected after posting, and the Siri-Tarino authors reported that studies not adjusting for blood cholesterol gave a result not significantly different from the full analysis, answering a criticism that adjustment had hidden an effect (V1, the authors' own check). Second, analyses that specify the replacement find lower risk when polyunsaturated fat, and to a lesser degree monounsaturated fat or whole grains, replaces saturated fat. Several (Wang, Zhang, Zong) draw on the same Harvard cohorts (the Nurses' Health Studies and the Health Professionals Follow-up Study) as displayed, and Li probably does too (not displayed), so they are not independent replications. Third, the Prospective Urban Rural Epidemiology (PURE) study, a different and geographically broader cohort, found lower stroke and total-mortality risk at higher saturated-fat intake; its authors read high carbohydrate intake as the harmful exposure (V1).

Cohort estimates carry the usual limits of observational data (measurement error in food questionnaires, healthy-user bias, reverse causality, confounding), which were **NOT SEARCHED**, so no evidence in this run addresses them directly. One comparison is telling: the Harvard-cohort estimate for swapping 5% of energy from saturated to polyunsaturated fat (total mortality HR 0.73) is far larger than the trial death estimates (RR 0.93 to 0.96, intervals including 1). The ledgers do not explain the gap; it is consistent with confounding, with differences in the size and duration of dietary change, or both.

**Low-carbohydrate diets.** Two V1 items were retrieved: a UK Biobank cohort (2024) linking a low-carbohydrate, high-fat diet to higher LDL cholesterol and apolipoprotein B and to major adverse cardiovascular events (adjusted HR 2.18; 1.39 to 3.43), and an imaging study of selected "lean mass hyper-responders" (very high LDL cholesterol, high HDL cholesterol, low triglycerides; sample size not displayed) in which baseline coronary plaque, not LDL cholesterol, predicted plaque progression over one year. Neither isolates saturated fat, and no outcome trial of ketogenic or low-carbohydrate diets was retrieved.

### 4.4 Reconciling the evidence

Three features may explain much of the apparent disagreement; each has qualifications.

1. **Comparator.** Null pooled cohorts ask "high versus low saturated fat, whatever replaces it"; substitution cohorts ask "saturated fat versus polyunsaturated fat." The 2017 AHA advisory gives its own estimate that trials replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated oil cut cardiovascular disease by "approximately 30%," and states that replacing it with refined carbohydrates and sugars is not associated with lower rates (V1; Sacks et al., 2017). Evidence on the carbohydrate swap is thin: no cohort estimate for refined carbohydrate was retrieved, and Cochrane-derived texts of uncertain version conflict (one says carbohydrate "appears less useful," another that benefits "arose from replacing saturated fats with polyunsaturated fat or starchy foods," a third that it is unclear which replacement is better). Cochrane's subgroup estimates by replacement were not retrieved, and the ordering of monounsaturated fat and whole grains below polyunsaturated fat comes from cohorts only.
2. **Outcome.** Blood cholesterol is well established, combined events show a modest benefit, and deaths show little or no effect (moderate certainty).
3. **Population risk.** The *Annals* review reports benefit concentrated in higher-risk people. That is one review, its estimates cannot be tied to strata in the search output, and the pattern is contested: the Cochrane figures show no clear gradient (56 versus 53), the *JMA Journal* review pooled mostly secondary-prevention trials and found no significant difference, and an expert quoted by the Science Media Centre argued the benefit applies to the whole population. A constant relative effect would also give larger absolute benefits at higher baseline risk (a general property of relative effects, not a finding of this run).

Unresolved: why PURE differs from the Harvard cohorts, and why the trial syntheses select different trials. `[MATERIAL GAP]`: the causal case for LDL cholesterol, landmark trial reanalyses, pooled studies of circulating saturated fatty acids and coronary disease, and trial risk of bias were not searched, nor was any post-2020 Cochrane update.

## 5. Other health outcomes

Type 2 diabetes was searched (11 searches, not every planned item); the cancer entry rests on one umbrella review; the other domains were not reached.

**Type 2 diabetes (certainty: low).** In 23 prospective studies, saturated-fat intake was not associated with higher diabetes incidence: summary relative risk 0.95 (0.90 to 1.00) from 11 studies, with the authors writing that a harmful association "was not confirmed" and rating certainty "very low to moderate" (Neuenschwander et al., 2020; V2). An updated cohort meta-analysis reported a hazard ratio of 0.93 (0.84 to 1.03) for total saturated fat and inverse associations for myristic and lauric acid intake (V1). In short feeding trials, replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat lowered glucose, glycated hemoglobin, C-peptide and insulin-resistance markers (Imamura et al., 2016; direction V2, numbers V1); these are biomarker endpoints, not diabetes incidence. A separate umbrella review of diet and diabetes, which included no saturated-fat exposure, reported higher incidence with processed meat (RR 1.37; 1.22 to 1.54 per 50 g/day) and red meat (1.17; 1.08 to 1.26 per 100 g/day) (Neuenschwander et al., 2019; V1); that is a food signal and is kept apart from the saturated-fat analysis.

**Cancer (certainty: unknown).** A 2026 umbrella review of dietary fat and cancer reported that saturated-fat intake was "associated with higher risks of breast, gastric, liver, and esophageal cancers" (Fan et al., 2026; V2). Effect sizes and GRADE ratings were not displayed and the input is largely observational, so the finding cannot be graded.

**Not searched** `[MATERIAL GAP]`: liver fat; body weight and fat distribution; dementia and cognition; inflammation and the gut microbiome; pregnancy, children, older adults and frailty; and deaths outside cardiovascular disease. Absence of results is not absence of evidence.

## 6. Does the food matter?

Nutrient-level advice treats all saturated fat alike. The trials and cohorts retrieved suggest it may not, but the evidence is thinner than for the nutrient as a whole: no retrieved randomized trial measured heart attacks or deaths by food source, and cohort evidence cannot separate the fat from the rest of the food.

### 6.1 Dairy, cheese and butter

**Trials (blood cholesterol only).** In a four-week crossover trial of 92 adults, a cheese-rich diet gave lower LDL cholesterol than a butter-rich diet but higher than monounsaturated- or polyunsaturated-fat diets (Table 3; Brassard et al., 2017; V1). The cheese–butter gap was significant only in people with high baseline LDL cholesterol, so "cheese raises LDL cholesterol less than butter" is not the same claim as "cheese does not raise LDL cholesterol." A six-week crossover in 49 adults reported that cheese lowered total, LDL and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol relative to butter at equal fat content (Hjerpsted et al., 2011; V1). Regular-fat cheese did not differ from reduced-fat cheese on LDL cholesterol over 12 weeks, but the comparators were other cheeses or carbohydrate foods, not unsaturated fat (Raziani et al., 2016; headline V2). Proposed mechanisms such as calcium–fatty-acid soaps were not confirmed in any retrieved result `[MATERIAL GAP]`, and funding for these trials was not displayed.

**Cohorts.** A dose-response meta-analysis of 29 cohorts (938,465 people) found no association of total dairy or milk with mortality, coronary heart disease (CHD) or CVD (Guo et al., 2017; V1). In the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) cohort (316 events), saturated fat from dairy was associated with lower CVD risk, HR 0.79 per additional 5 g/day (de Oliveira Otto et al., 2012; V1). A global analysis in *Nature Communications* found cheese and low-fat dairy inversely associated with CVD, no significant association for milk, yogurt or high-fat dairy, and 9% higher CHD risk with regular dairy (mainly whole milk) among Chinese participants (V1). A Swedish study reported higher all-cause mortality with high milk intake (Michaëlsson et al., 2014; hazard ratios not displayed, V1).

**Biomarkers.** Circulating pentadecanoic (15:0) and heptadecanoic (17:0) acids, markers of dairy-fat intake, were inversely associated with CVD in a Swedish cohort and a meta-analysis (RR 0.88, 0.78–0.99 and 0.86, 0.79–0.93, highest versus lowest third) and not with all-cause mortality (Trieu et al., 2021; V1); a biomarker is not an intake and cannot separate dairy fat from other dairy components.

**Butter.** A meta-analysis of nine studies (636,151 people) found, per 14 g/day, all-cause mortality RR 1.01 (1.00–1.03), CVD RR 1.00 (0.98–1.02) and diabetes RR 0.96 (0.93–0.99), and concluded the data did not support a major guideline emphasis on raising or lowering butter intake (Pimpin et al., 2016; V1). In large US cohorts the highest butter intake went with 15% higher total mortality than the lowest (HR 1.15; 1.08–1.22), higher cancer mortality but not CVD mortality; replacing butter with plant-based oils was associated with lower mortality (HR 0.83) (Zhang et al., 2025; HR 1.15 and 0.83 are V2, the rest V1). One output describes the substitution as 10 g/day of butter, another as each 5 g/day of plant oil, so the contrast could not be settled. Butter and plant oils differ in more than saturated fat, and why the two studies differ was not established.

### 6.2 Meat

In the UK Biobank (474,985 adults), processed meat was associated with ischemic heart disease, HR 1.09 (1.04–1.15) per 20 g/day, and unprocessed red plus processed meat with HR 1.15 (1.07–1.23) per 70 g/day; higher body mass index explained a substantial share of the associations (Papier et al., 2021; design V2, estimates V1). A systematic review of randomized trials found that diets lower in red meat may have little or no effect on all-cause mortality (HR 0.99; 0.95–1.03) and cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.98; 0.91–1.06), with low to very low certainty (Zeraatkar et al., 2019; V1). In a feeding trial, red and white meat raised LDL cholesterol and apolipoprotein B relative to non-meat protein regardless of saturated-fat level (Bergeron et al., 2019; Table 3). The Astrup critique argues that unprocessed red meat is not linked to heart disease and that its saturated-fat content is "unlikely to be responsible" for risk. Which meat components drive risk (saturated fat, sodium, nitrite, heme iron) was **NOT SEARCHED** `[MATERIAL GAP]`.

### 6.3 Tropical oils and individual fatty acids

Coconut oil raised LDL cholesterol relative to non-tropical vegetable oils across 17 trials (Neelakantan et al., 2020) and did not differ from olive oil in one small trial whose interval could not rule out a difference of about 0.2 mmol/L either way (Khaw et al., 2018; both in Table 3). Palm oil, palm kernel oil, medium-chain oils, cocoa butter, stearic acid and very-long-chain saturated fatty acids were **NOT SEARCHED** `[MATERIAL GAP]`. Two cohort summaries on individual acids point in different directions: in the two Harvard cohorts, higher intakes of lauric, myristic, palmitic and stearic acid went with 7%, 13%, 18% and 18% higher CHD incidence, while replacing 1% of energy from these acids with polyunsaturated fat, monounsaturated fat or whole grains went with 8%, 5% and 6% lower CHD (Zong et al., 2016; V1, exposure contrast not displayed); in a diabetes meta-analysis, higher myristic and lauric acid intakes were inversely associated with incidence (V1).

### 6.4 What the food evidence adds up to

In the trials retrieved, cheese raised LDL cholesterol less than butter in two small trials (Hjerpsted et al., 2011; Brassard et al., 2017) and coconut oil less than butter in one (Khaw et al., 2018), but cheese still raised it more than monounsaturated- and polyunsaturated-fat diets (Brassard et al., 2017), and coconut oil did not differ from olive oil in a small trial that cannot rule out a difference. No retrieved trial shows whether these differences change rates of heart attacks or deaths. The Nutrition Evidence Systematic Review (NESR) concluded that swapping red or processed meat for dairy, whole grains or vegetables goes with lower cardiovascular morbidity and that swapping one form of dairy for another makes no difference (both graded moderate on NESR's own scale; V1 each); the study types it included were not displayed. Ultra-processed food was barely reached: one umbrella review linked it to 32 adverse health outcomes with no result on saturated fat (Lane et al., 2024; V1, press summary), so whether processing, rather than the fat, explains associations for foods such as processed meat is unknown.

## 7. Natural versus artificial

The short answer is that the retrieved evidence supports a clear contrast for trans fat, does not test whether a saturated fatty acid's origin matters, and does not reach most other "artificial" fats.

**What can differ between two fats:** double-bond geometry (the defining feature of trans fat), the position of fatty acids on the glycerol backbone, the food matrix, co-ingredients and process contaminants. Retrieved evidence addressed geometry and, in cheese-versus-butter trials, the matrix; position (relevant to interesterification), refining and heating contaminants, and molecular identity were **NOT SEARCHED**.

### 7.1 Industrial trans fat (R-b): the clear case

Trans fat is an unsaturated fat in which at least one double bond is in the trans configuration (Mozaffarian et al., 2006; V1); it is not a saturated fat. The industrial source is partially hydrogenated oil.

- **Cohorts.** A pooled analysis of four cohorts found RR 1.23 (1.11–1.37) for CHD per 2% of energy from trans fat (Mozaffarian et al., 2006; V1). A meta-analysis of observational studies found total trans fat associated with all-cause mortality (RR 1.34; 1.16–1.56; V2), CHD mortality (1.28; 1.09–1.50) and total CHD (1.21; 1.10–1.33) (de Souza et al., 2015; V1). Industrial trans fat had CHD RR 1.42 (1.05–1.92) and CHD mortality 1.18 (1.04–1.33); an earlier meta-analysis found the industrial estimate not statistically significant, RR 1.21 (0.97–1.50) (Bendsen et al., 2011; V1).
- **Feeding trials.** In a quantitative review of 39 studies, each 1% of energy from industrial trans fat replacing cis-monounsaturated fat raised the LDL:HDL ratio by 0.053, after a published correction from 0.055; the authors said the correction did not change their conclusion (Brouwer et al., 2010; original V2, corrected V1).
- **Regulation and population effects.** The FDA determined in June 2015 that partially hydrogenated oils are not generally recognized as safe and issued a rule effective 22 December 2023 revoking remaining uses; trans fat remains naturally in meat and dairy (Food and Drug Administration, 2015; V2). WHO modelling attributes more than 278,000 deaths a year to industrial trans fat (World Health Organization, 2024a; V2), and WHO certified countries for elimination in January 2024 and 2025 (World Health Organization, 2024b; News-Medical, 2025; Resolve to Save Lives, 2025; V2; the last is an advocacy group). Population evidence is mixed and ecological: Denmark's 2004 limit was followed by about 14.2 fewer CVD deaths per 100,000 per year relative to a synthetic control (Restrepo & Rieger, 2016; V1), New York counties with restrictions saw a 6.2% greater decline in heart attack and stroke admissions (Brandt et al., 2017; V1), and Austria's 2009 regulation showed no significant change in the CVD mortality trend (V1).

Certainty that industrial trans fat is harmful: **high** for its effect on blood lipids (several feeding trials; original review V2) and **moderate** for clinical outcomes (most cohort estimates are significant, one meta-analysis found the industrial estimate not significant, and the policy studies are ecological and mixed). No evidence of benefit from industrial trans fat was found.

### 7.2 Natural (ruminant) trans fat (R-d): "natural" does not mean "neutral"

- **Cohorts.** Ruminant trans fat was not significantly associated with CHD: RR 0.93 (0.73–1.18) for CHD and 1.01 (0.71–1.43) for CHD mortality (de Souza et al., 2015), and RR 0.92 (0.76–1.11) in the earlier meta-analysis (Bendsen et al., 2011), all V1. The intervals are wide, so modest harm is not excluded. Circulating trans-palmitoleic acid, a dairy-fat marker, was inversely associated with new-onset diabetes in 3,736 older adults (hazard ratios 0.41 and 0.38 in upper fifths; exact contrast not displayed; Mozaffarian et al., 2010; V1), but a biomarker association does not show that eating it is protective.
- **Feeding trials.** The lipid effect looks similar per gram: the quantitative review found no significant difference between ruminant and industrial trans fat (p = 0.37), and a later review by the same group concluded that "gram for gram, all trans fatty acids have largely the same effect on blood lipoproteins" (Brouwer et al., 2010, original V2; Brouwer et al., 2013, V1). A double-blind trial of 106 adults found that 3.3% of energy as vaccenic acid (the main ruminant trans fat) raised total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, the total:HDL ratio and apolipoprotein B by 2% to 6% relative to a stearic-acid control diet, as industrial trans fat did, and also raised HDL cholesterol, apolipoprotein A-I and lipoprotein(a) (Gebauer et al., 2015; numbers V1, qualitative result V2). The earlier TRANSFACT trial reported that HDL lowering was specific to industrial sources and that LDL rose with natural sources in women but not men (Chardigny et al., 2008; V1). A meta-regression of 13 trials found no linear relationship between ruminant trans fat and lipid ratios over 0.1% to 4.2% of energy (Gayet-Boyer et al., 2014; V1; funding not displayed, conflict-of-interest check advised).
- **Industry funding.** A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis (10 trials; 12 cohorts) concluded that trans fats from dairy foods do not affect cardiometabolic disease risk. The US National Dairy Council funded it, two authors are affiliated with dairy bodies, and a corrigendum exists whose content was not read (Gayet-Boyer et al., 2026; funding V2). Declared funding does not show a conclusion is wrong, but the review's numbers are provisional until the corrigendum is read.
- **Dose and intake.** Trial doses and cohort intake ranges differ, and habitual intake data were **NOT SEARCHED**, so the two kinds of evidence cannot be reconciled here. The WHO limit of less than 1% of energy applies to industrial and ruminant trans fat together (World Health Organization, 2024a; V2; the full guideline text was not retrieved).

Judgment: the feeding trials suggest ruminant trans fat is not lipid-neutral per gram (certainty low: small trials, partly conflicting results, one dairy-funded review pointing the other way). Population harm is unresolved: cohort estimates are not significant, with wide intervals.

### 7.3 Not searched, and one regulatory fact (R-a, R-b, R-c)

**Same fatty acid, different origin (R-a).** No retrieved study isolates the origin of a saturated fatty acid. The trials in Sections 4 and 6 compare different compositions and foods, so they cannot say whether a fatty acid made in a factory behaves differently from the same fatty acid in butter. `[MATERIAL GAP]`

**Other modified fats and substitutes (R-b, R-c).** Not reached; the questions are in `resume_plan.md`: whether interesterification of palmitic- and stearic-rich fats changes lipids or blood glucose; whether fully hydrogenated fat (mostly stearic acid) behaves like other saturated fat; what is known of olestra, salatrim and diacylglycerol oil; how refining and frying contaminants relate to health; and fermentation- and CO2-derived fats. `[MATERIAL GAP]`

**Cultivated fat (R-c).** Cell-cultivated pork fat made by Mission Barns received an FDA "no further questions" response and US Department of Agriculture clearance "enabling its first products to enter the US market" on 24 July 2025 (existence V2, date V1). That is a safety-status clearance, not a nutrition study: no data on cardiometabolic effects or fatty-acid composition were seen.

### 7.4 "Natural" animal fats versus "seed oils" (R-f)

Popular framing sets butter, tallow and coconut oil ("natural") against soybean, canola and sunflower oils ("industrial"). In trials, butter and coconut oil raised LDL cholesterol relative to unsaturated oils (Table 3). Tallow was not tested in any retrieved trial; a 2026 clinician's guide says it is "consistently linked to increased LDL cholesterol versus unsaturated oils" and lists seed oils among items with evidence of cardiovascular benefit (Miller et al., 2026; V1, machine summary). In large US cohorts each 5 g/day of canola, soybean or olive oil went with lower total mortality (HR 0.85, 0.94 and 0.92; V1; order as displayed; Zhang et al., 2025). The primary literature on linoleic acid, inflammation and cardiovascular outcomes, and the reanalyses of the Sydney and Minnesota trials, were **NOT SEARCHED** `[MATERIAL GAP]`, so whether claims about seed oils have scientific support beyond these few items is not assessed.

## 8. Controversy, funding and bias

**History and framing.** Internal industry documents showed that the Sugar Research Foundation sponsored its first coronary heart disease research project in 1965, paying the Harvard scientists the equivalent of about $48,000 to $50,000 in 2016 dollars (the two outputs disagree on the figure); the funding was not publicly disclosed, although the *New England Journal of Medicine* has required disclosures since 1984 (V1), and the authors found no direct evidence that the industry wrote or changed the review (Kearns et al., 2016; V2). This documents industry influence on how the debate was framed, not whether saturated fat is harmful.

**The two sides of the current debate.** The Astrup review argues that meta-analyses show "no beneficial effects" of reducing saturated fat on cardiovascular disease and total mortality (its displayed abstract names no replacement nutrient), that the rise in LDL cholesterol is in larger LDL particles which the authors say are much less strongly related to cardiovascular risk, and that recommendations should be food-based rather than nutrient-based (Astrup et al., 2020; V2). The 2017 AHA advisory argues the opposite (Sacks et al., 2017; V1). A structured debate in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* had Kris-Etherton arguing for and Krauss against reducing saturated fat as much as possible (Krauss & Kris-Etherton, 2020; V1, press summaries). Neither side's displayed text shows the trial sets behind its claim, so the conflict cannot be traced to specific trials from this run.

**Declared interests.** As summarized in one search output (V1; to be re-checked against the journal), the Astrup review's disclosures include research funding or honoraria from dairy bodies, the National Cattlemen's Beef Association and the Malaysian Palm Board, and ties to ketogenic-diet companies; three authors declared nothing. One author who declared dairy funding there also co-authored two studies cited here (Siri-Tarino et al., 2010; Bergeron et al., 2019), whose declared interests were not displayed; funding statements were not displayed for most cited papers. Declared relationships are not evidence about whether a conclusion is right. The interests of the opposing documents' authors, pharmaceutical ties, advocacy groups and agencies were **NOT SEARCHED**, so this record is one-sided by search coverage, not by finding.

**Meta-research on funding** is mixed and not specific to saturated fat: one study found an odds ratio (OR) of 7.61 (1.27–45.73) for a favorable conclusion in industry-funded nutrition articles, a systematic review of 12 reports a smaller, non-significant difference (RR 1.31; 0.99–1.72) (Lesser et al., 2007; Chartres et al., 2016; V1).


## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 What is established, contested and unknown

**Supported.** LDL cholesterol is higher on butter than on olive oil and higher on coconut oil than on non-tropical vegetable oils, and industrial trans fat worsens blood lipids (high certainty; blood measures; Table 3). Reducing or modifying saturated fat, with mixed replacement nutrients, modestly lowers combined cardiovascular events, with little or no effect on deaths (Cochrane's own ratings: moderate), and industrial trans fat harms clinical outcomes (moderate).

**Low certainty or contested.** A benefit specific to polyunsaturated fat; benefit concentrated in higher-risk people; food-source differences in outcomes; whether reducing saturated fat lowers deaths; whether dairy fat is neutral or protective; whether the *Annals* review supports "nuance" or continued restriction; no harmful association of total saturated fat with type 2 diabetes in cohorts; and the lipid effects and harmlessness of ruminant trans fat.

**Unknown or not searched.** See Section 10.1 and Appendix A.

### 9.2 What this means in practice

This is general information, not personal medical advice. Children, pregnancy, older adults, familial hypercholesterolemia and lipid-lowering medicines were not searched, so nobody should change treatment or diet on the basis of this report. What the retrieved evidence points toward:

- **The swap, not just the cut.** Benefit was seen when saturated fat was reduced or modified with mixed replacements; a polyunsaturated-specific event result rests on one review, and no food-level swap was tested on heart attacks or deaths.
- **The food counts.** Cheese raised LDL cholesterol less than butter but more than unsaturated fat in trials, and processed meat is consistently associated with harm in cohorts. The label "natural" did not predict the LDL response.
- **Industrial trans fat is the clear artificial hazard.** Check ingredient lists for partially hydrogenated oils where they are still permitted. Natural trans fat raised LDL cholesterol per gram in feeding trials; its habitual intakes were not searched.
- **Baseline risk may matter.** One review found the benefit concentrated in people at higher baseline cardiovascular risk (definition not displayed) over five years, with little in low-risk people; other results do not show the gradient. People with existing heart disease or other risk factors should discuss diet with a clinician.

### 9.3 Limitations of this review

1. **Search and access.** The shared allowance of 200 web searches ended every stream early, and full texts could not be fetched (Section 2).
2. **Machine summaries.** Every number is a machine-written summary of an abstract or page. Summaries blend pages, sometimes repeat numbers placed in the query, and in several cases attached figures to the wrong paper (for example, 12 trials and 53,758 participants were attached to the *Annals* review but belong to the 2020 Cochrane review); such outputs were discarded. V1 and V2 measure transcription fidelity, not independent confirmation.
3. **Coverage and bias.** Foundational lipid meta-analyses, genetic evidence on LDL cholesterol, landmark trials and several outcome domains were not searched; no study-level risk-of-bias appraisal was made; several US cohort papers share participants; recent items were found more easily than older work; and funding statements were mostly not displayed (Section 8).
4. **AI assistance.** Eight AI search agents extracted evidence, one AI session synthesized and drafted it, and six AI reviewers examined the plan, synthesis and drafts. No human expert has reviewed the report.

## 10. Gaps, next steps and conclusion

### 10.1 Gaps and next steps

Coverage by sub-question is mapped in Appendix A. In priority order, the gaps that most change the answer are: the unsearched "artificial" fats (interesterification, hydrogenation, fat substitutes, refining contaminants, fermentation-derived fats and the molecular-identity question); the foundations of the cholesterol argument and landmark diet–heart trials; liver fat, cognition, inflammation and cancer; other national and WHO guidance; susceptibility, low-carbohydrate diets and the primary seed-oil literature; and verification of the headline numbers against abstracts.

Finishing needs three changes outside this session, which are the user's to make: raise the `CLAUDE_CODE_MAX_WEB_SEARCHES_PER_SESSION` setting; optionally allow the blocked scholarly hosts; and start a new session pointing at `resume_plan.md`, which gives the steps and the prioritized searches (roughly 250 to 350 further searches by the streams' own estimates).

### 10.2 Conclusion

On the evidence retrieved, "saturated fat is bad for you" and "saturated fat is fine" are both oversimplifications. Butter and coconut oil raise LDL cholesterol relative to unsaturated oils. In trials that reduced or modified saturated fat, combined cardiovascular events fell by about 17% while deaths did not clearly change; whether the benefit is larger in higher-risk people, or specific to polyunsaturated fat, rests on single reviews and is contested. The food the fat comes in affects cholesterol. On natural versus artificial, the clear finding concerns trans fat: the industrial form is harmful, and the natural form is not lipid-neutral per gram. Most other "artificial" fats, and whether a saturated fatty acid's origin matters, were not searched. The numbers need re-verification against full texts.
