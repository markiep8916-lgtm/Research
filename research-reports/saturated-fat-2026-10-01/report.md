# Saturated Fat and Health: What the Evidence Shows, and Whether Natural and Artificial Fats Differ

*Interim deep-research report (full mode) · prepared 1 October 2026 · search-limited · assisted by artificial intelligence (AI)*

> **Read this first.** This report was built from web-search results only. Every number comes from a machine-written search summary of an abstract or journal page, not from a full text. ## Key takeaways

1. **The answer is conditional, and the condition is what replaces the fat.** Trials and cohort studies agree on a structure: swapping saturated fat for polyunsaturated fat (and, to a lesser degree, monounsaturated fat or whole grains) goes with lower heart-disease risk, whereas swapping it for refined carbohydrate, or leaving the replacement unspecified, shows little. Certainty: low to moderate.
2. **The cholesterol effect is well established.** In controlled trials, replacing butter, beef tallow or tropical oils with unsaturated plant oils lowers low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and every professional body retrieved accepts this. Certainty: high.
3. **Heart-disease events: a modest benefit, concentrated in higher-risk people.** The 2020 Cochrane review pooled 12 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and found about 17% fewer combined cardiovascular events when saturated fat was reduced: a relative risk (RR) of 0.83, 95% confidence interval 0.70 to 0.98, moderate certainty, with no clear effect on deaths. A December 2025 review in *Annals of Internal Medicine* (17 trials, 66,337 adults) found the benefit concentrated in people at high baseline risk and little or none over five years in people at low risk. A separate 2025 meta-analysis of nine trials found no significant differences. Certainty: moderate for combined events, low for deaths.
4. **The food matters, not just the nutrient.** Cheese raised LDL cholesterol less than butter in trials, but more than unsaturated fats. Dairy as a group is neutral or inversely associated with heart disease in cohorts. Butter was linked to somewhat higher mortality than plant oils in large US cohorts. Processed meat is consistently associated with harm. Coconut oil raises LDL cholesterol relative to non-tropical vegetable oils. Certainty: low to moderate; no trial measured heart attacks or deaths by food source.
5. **Natural versus artificial: clear for trans fat, unresolved for everything else.** Industrial trans fat is clearly harmful, but it is an *unsaturated* fat, not a saturated one. Natural (ruminant) trans fat is not lipid-neutral gram for gram, although cohort studies show no clear harm at usual intakes, and the most favourable recent review (on dairy trans fat) was funded by the US National Dairy Council. For saturated fat itself, nothing retrieved suggests that the *origin* of a fatty acid (natural or made in a factory) changes its effect; what differs is the replacement, the food and the processing. Interesterified fats, fully hydrogenated fats, fat substitutes and lab-grown fats were **NOT SEARCHED**.
6. **Official guidance is split.** The 2025–2030 US Dietary Guidelines for Americans (DGA), released in January 2026, keep the cap of 10% of calories from saturated fat but list meats, full-fat dairy, butter and beef tallow among acceptable fats. The American Heart Association (AHA) says that food advice could lead people past the limit, and its March 2026 statement advises unsaturated fat in place of saturated fat.
7. **Not reached.** Liver fat, cancer (beyond one umbrella review), dementia, inflammation, children and older adults, genetic susceptibility, "seed oil" claims, palm oil, individual fatty acids and the foundational cholesterol meta-analyses were **NOT SEARCHED** or only touched.

## Abstract

**Background:** Dietary guidance on saturated fat is contested, and popular claims contrast "natural" fats with "artificial" or industrial ones. **Purpose:** To summarise the best available evidence on the health effects of saturated fat and on whether natural and artificial fats differ. **Method:** A rapid, systematic-style narrative review, run as eight parallel search streams with adversarial review. Searching was limited to a shared allowance of 200 web searches, and full texts could not be fetched, so findings come from search summaries and are graded by how well they were seen. **Findings:** Replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat lowers low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and, in randomized trials, modestly reduces combined cardiovascular events (relative risk 0.83, 95% confidence interval 0.70 to 0.98), with benefit concentrated in higher-risk people and no clear effect on deaths. Cohort studies agree only when the replacement nutrient is specified. Food source matters: cheese raises cholesterol less than butter, and processed meat is consistently associated with harm. Industrial trans fat, an unsaturated fat, is clearly harmful; natural trans fat is not lipid-neutral per gram. No evidence was retrieved that the origin of a saturated fatty acid changes its effect. Interesterified fats, fat substitutes, lab-grown fats and most non-cardiovascular outcomes were not searched. **Implications:** Guidance should name the replacement and the food. A verification pass with full-text access is needed before the numbers are relied on. **Keywords:** saturated fat; trans fat; cardiovascular disease; dietary guidelines; food matrix; evidence review.

## 1. Introduction and scope

Few nutrition questions have been argued as long as the health effects of saturated fat, and the argument changed shape in 2026. In January the US government's new dietary guidelines kept a numeric limit on saturated fat while naming butter and beef tallow among acceptable cooking fats. In March the AHA published guidance that advises a different emphasis (Lichtenstein et al., 2026; U.S. Department of Health and Human Services & U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2026). Meanwhile a December 2025 review of randomized trials was reportedly read in opposite ways by its own authors and by the journal's editorialists (Steen et al., 2025/2026; Estruch & Lamuela-Raventós, 2026). Against that background, the question asked was: *what does the best available evidence show about the health effects of dietary saturated fat, and do those effects depend on whether the fat is "natural" or "artificial"?*

**What "artificial versus natural" can mean.** The phrase is ambiguous, so every plausible reading was put in scope. Table 1 shows what each reading asks and how far this run answered it.

## 2. Method

**Design.** A rapid evidence review in the style of a systematic review, not a registered Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) review, and it should not be described as one. Eight search streams ran in parallel (guidelines and policy; blood lipids and randomized trials; cohort studies; non-cardiovascular outcomes; food sources and individual fatty acids; natural versus artificial fats; controversy and susceptibility; a 2025–2026 recency sweep). A Devil's Advocate reviewed the plan before searching and the synthesis afterwards, and the draft report received an independent claim audit (Appendix C).

**Search channel and its limits.** Only the web-search tool worked. The session's allowance of 200 searches was shared by all agents, and the eight streams used 196 of them (the dispatcher used four): 30, 17, 30, 11, 31, 32, 16 and 29 searches respectively. Every stream was cut off before finishing. Fetching full texts from PubMed, PubMed Central, Europe PMC, doi.org, BMJ, the Cochrane Library, AHA journals, Nature, WHO, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and similar sites was blocked by the network policy. No primary document was opened.

**What the evidence labels mean.** A *V2* label means the same fact appeared in at least two independent search outputs; *V1* means one output; *V0* means the agent knew it from background memory without seeing it, and V0 items were never used as findings. Search summaries are machine-written, can blend several pages, and sometimes repeat numbers that were placed in the query. Where a number appeared only after being typed into a query, it was not counted as confirmation. The labels therefore measure transcription fidelity, not independent replication. The Devil's Advocate raised this point and it is adopted throughout.

**Grading.** Sources were graded on the seven-level hierarchy in the deep-research skill (systematic reviews and meta-analyses highest; expert opinion and guidelines lowest) and, where the authors reported it, by the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) certainty rating. Comparators are always stated: replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat, with monounsaturated fat, with carbohydrate and with an unspecified replacement are different questions.

**Handling of gaps.** Unsearched domains are reported as **NOT SEARCHED**. Where a section would need information the evidence base does not contain, the text carries `[MATERIAL GAP]` instead of filling from memory.

## 3. Background and what authorities say

Saturated fatty acids (SFA) are fatty acids with no carbon–carbon double bonds. Trans fatty acids (TFA), by contrast, are unsaturated fatty acids with at least one double bond in the trans configuration (Mozaffarian et al., 2006, V1). That distinction matters for the rest of this report, because industrial trans fat is often lumped in with saturated fat in popular discussion but is a different kind of molecule. The chemistry of individual saturated fatty acids, the composition of common fats and oils, and population intake data were **NOT SEARCHED** `[MATERIAL GAP]`.

### 3.2 The 2026 US dispute

Three positions can be documented without taking a side. The US Secretary of Health and Human Services (HHS) described the guidelines as "ending the war on saturated fats" (The Hill, 2026; V2). The AHA and the American College of Cardiology (ACC) welcomed the cap but raised concerns about the food advice, and the Harvard T.H. **What the Cochrane review found.** Reducing saturated fat for at least two years (average about 4.7 years) lowered combined cardiovascular events by 17% in the 2020 abstract (RR 0.83; V2). The original 2015 version reported the same point estimate with a slightly different interval (0.72 to 0.96; 13 comparisons; 53,300 participants; Hooper et al., 2015; V2). A third set of numbers (RR 0.79; 0.66 to 0.93; 11 trials; 53,300 participants) appears in a New Zealand Cochrane summary document and in a snippet of a 2024 umbrella review. It matches neither abstract, and its version could not be resolved, so it is not used as the Cochrane estimate. The review's own meta-regression suggested greater reductions in saturated fat were associated with greater reductions in events (V1). The effect on deaths was small and compatible with none, and the effect on individual events was rated very low quality.

**What the 2025 *Annals* review added.** It stratified trials by baseline cardiovascular risk. For people at low risk, absolute reductions fell below the authors' thresholds of importance (5 and 10 per 1,000 people over five years for fatal and non-fatal outcomes); for people at high risk they exceeded them (V2). Eleven of the 17 trials replaced saturated fat mainly with polyunsaturated fat, four with carbohydrate and one with monounsaturated fat (V1; the counts sum to 16). Most displayed confidence intervals include 1.0; the authors' "may reduce" wording rests on their certainty framework and absolute-risk thresholds. The review reported no external funding (V1).

**A dispute over interpretation.** The accompanying editorial argued for a "nuanced" message; a news summary reports that it read the findings as showing saturated fats are unlikely to be harmful for the general population, contrary to the reviewers' own conclusions, and that the reviewers objected to the journal (V1, secondary). Professor Nita Forouhi of the University of Cambridge, quoted by the Science Media Centre, said the authors had made some methodological improvements but "introduced new methodological challenges that limit interpretation" (V1). The editorial's text and any journal response were not read.

**Why the JMA Journal review differs.** It pooled fewer trials (9, against 12 and 17), searched only to April 2023, and concluded that reducing saturated fat "cannot be recommended at present" (V2). The displayed text does not say which trials it excluded; trial selection is a plausible explanation but is unverified. Its null result on mortality is compatible with the Cochrane and *Annals* mortality estimates, whose intervals also include 1.0. The reviews therefore disagree less on deaths than on the composite endpoint and on how to frame the result.

Three patterns stand out. First, pooled analyses with an unspecified replacement are consistently null, and the authors of the best-known one rated the evidence "very low" certainty (de Souza et al., 2015). Second, analyses that specify the replacement find lower risk when polyunsaturated fat, and to a lesser degree monounsaturated fat or whole grains, replaces saturated fat. Several of these (Wang, Zhang, Zong and probably Li) draw on the same two Harvard cohorts, so they are not independent replications. Third, the Prospective Urban Rural Epidemiology (PURE) study, a different and geographically broader cohort, found *lower* stroke and total-mortality risk at higher saturated-fat intake. The displayed text does not explain the divergence from the Harvard cohorts; the ledgers record critiques of PURE as titles only (V1). An authors' sensitivity analysis answered a criticism that adjusting for serum cholesterol had hidden an effect in the Siri-Tarino meta-analysis: studies that did not adjust gave a result not significantly different (V1, the original authors' own check).

### 4.4 Reconciling the evidence

Three features account for most of the apparent disagreement, and each is documented above.

1. **Comparator.** Null pooled cohorts ask "high versus low saturated fat, whatever replaces it". Trials and substitution cohorts ask "saturated fat versus polyunsaturated fat". The 2017 AHA advisory states that replacing saturated fat with refined carbohydrates and sugars is not associated with lower cardiovascular rates, and that trials replacing it with polyunsaturated oil cut cardiovascular disease by "approximately 30%" (V1 for both statements; Sacks et al., 2017). The critique paper by Astrup and colleagues says reducing saturated fat has "no beneficial effects" on cardiovascular disease and total mortality, but the abstract as displayed names no replacement nutrient (V2; Astrup et al., 2020).
2. **Outcome.** Cholesterol is well established; combined events show a modest benefit; individual events and deaths are not distinguishable from no effect in the pooled estimates.
3. **Population risk.** Benefit appears concentrated in higher-risk people. Absolute numbers support that reading: the Cochrane review's number needed to treat was 56 in primary prevention, and the *Annals* review judged low-risk absolute effects to be below its thresholds.

Two issues are unresolved: why PURE differs from the Harvard cohorts, and why the trial syntheses select different trials. `[MATERIAL GAP]`: the causal case for LDL cholesterol (genetic and drug trials), landmark trial re-analyses, and biomarker studies of circulating fatty acids were not searched.

## 5. Other health outcomes

Only type 2 diabetes was searched thoroughly; the other domains were not reached.

**Type 2 diabetes (certainty: low).** In 23 prospective studies, saturated-fat intake was not associated with higher diabetes incidence: summary relative risk 0.95 (0.90 to 1.00) from 11 studies, with the authors writing that a harmful association "was not confirmed" and rating certainty "very low to moderate" (Neuenschwander et al., 2020; V2). An updated meta-analysis of cohorts reported hazard ratio 0.93 (0.84 to 1.03) for total saturated fat and inverse associations for myristic and lauric acids (V1). In short controlled-feeding trials, replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat lowered glucose, glycated haemoglobin, C-peptide and insulin-resistance markers, while replacing carbohydrate with saturated fat left fasting glucose unchanged (+0.02 mmol/L; −0.01 to +0.04) and lowered fasting insulin slightly (Imamura et al., 2016; direction V2, numbers V1). These are biomarker endpoints under isocaloric swaps, not diabetes incidence, and the two bodies of evidence answer different questions. Red and processed meat, not saturated fat, carried the diabetes signal in an umbrella review (processed meat RR 1.37 per 50 g/day; V1).

**Cancer (certainty: unknown).** A 2026 umbrella review of dietary fat and cancer reported that saturated-fat intake was "associated with higher risks of breast, gastric, liver, and esophageal cancers" (Fan et al., 2026; V2). Effect sizes and GRADE ratings were not displayed, the input is largely observational, and the finding cannot be graded from what was retrieved. The International Agency for Research on Cancer classifies processed meat as carcinogenic (Group 1) and red meat as probably carcinogenic (Group 2A), which describes the strength of evidence about *meat*, not the size of risk and not saturated fat (V2). The Cochrane review's 2015 version reported no evidence of harm from reducing saturated fat on cancer mortality or diagnoses (V1).

**Not searched:** liver fat and fatty liver disease; body weight and fat distribution; cognition and dementia; inflammation, endotoxaemia and the gut microbiome; pregnancy, children, older adults and frailty; and deaths outside cardiovascular disease. These are marked **NOT SEARCHED**; do not read the absence of results as absence of evidence. `[MATERIAL GAP]`

## 6. Does the food matter?

Nutrient-level advice treats all saturated fat alike. The trials and cohorts retrieved suggest it should not, but the evidence is thinner than for the nutrient as a whole: no randomized trial measured heart attacks or deaths by food source, and the cohort evidence cannot separate the fat from the rest of the food.

### 6.1 Dairy, cheese and butter

**Trials (blood cholesterol only).** In a four-week crossover trial of 92 adults, a cheese-rich saturated-fat diet gave LDL cholesterol 3.3% lower than a butter-rich diet but 5.3% higher than a monounsaturated-fat diet and 12.3% higher than a polyunsaturated-fat diet (Brassard et al., 2017; V1). The cheese–butter gap was significant only in people with high baseline LDL cholesterol. "Cheese raises LDL cholesterol less than butter" is therefore not the same claim as "cheese does not raise LDL cholesterol". A six-week crossover in 49 adults reported that cheese lowered total, LDL and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol relative to butter at equal fat content (Hjerpsted et al., 2011; V1). Regular-fat cheese did not differ from reduced-fat cheese on LDL cholesterol over 12 weeks, but the comparators were other cheeses or carbohydrate foods, not unsaturated fat (Raziani et al., 2016; headline V2). A 164-person trial reported a larger LDL reduction when dairy fat was eaten as cheese than as a formulated product, which hints that physical structure matters; no numbers were displayed and the mechanism was not tested (Feeney et al., 2018; V1). Proposed mechanisms such as calcium–fatty-acid soaps were not confirmed in any retrieved result `[MATERIAL GAP]`.

**Cohorts.** A dose-response meta-analysis of 29 cohorts (938,465 people) found no association of total dairy or milk with mortality, CHD or CVD (Guo et al., 2017; V1). In the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) cohort (316 events), saturated fat from dairy was associated with lower CVD risk, HR 0.79 per additional 5 g/day (de Oliveira Otto et al., 2012; V1). A global analysis published in *Nature Communications* found cheese and low-fat dairy inversely associated with CVD and no significant association for milk, yogurt or high-fat dairy; among Chinese participants, regular dairy (mainly whole milk) went with 9% higher CHD risk (V1). A Swedish study reported higher all-cause mortality with high milk intake (Michaëlsson et al., 2014; hazard ratios not displayed, V1). A 2016 systematic review concluded there was "no evidence that the consumption of any form of dairy is detrimentally associated with any cardiovascular-related clinical outcome", a weaker claim than "protective" (Drouin-Chartier et al., 2016; V1). Press-derived percentages from the PURE dairy paper were not used because the Lancet abstract was not seen.

**Biomarkers.** Circulating pentadecanoic (15:0) and heptadecanoic (17:0) acids, which are markers of dairy-fat intake, were inversely associated with CVD in a Swedish cohort and a meta-analysis: relative risk 0.88 (0.78–0.99) and 0.86 (0.79–0.93), highest versus lowest third; there was no association with all-cause mortality (Trieu et al., 2021; V1). A biomarker is not an intake, and it cannot separate dairy fat from other dairy components. Odd-chain biomarkers were also inversely related to type 2 diabetes in a 2025 meta-analysis (relative risk 0.68 and 0.64; V1).

**Butter.** A meta-analysis of nine studies (636,151 people) found, per 14 g/day, all-cause mortality RR 1.01 (1.00–1.03), CVD RR 1.00 (0.98–1.02) and diabetes RR 0.96 (0.93–0.99), and concluded the data did not support a major guideline emphasis on raising or lowering butter intake (Pimpin et al., 2016; V1). Large US cohorts reported the highest butter intake associated with 15% higher total mortality than the lowest (HR 1.15; 1.08–1.22), higher cancer mortality but not CVD mortality; replacing butter with plant-based oils was associated with lower mortality (HR 0.83), strongest for soybean, canola and olive oils (Zhang et al., 2025; HR 1.15 and 0.83 are V2). One summary describes the substitution as 10 g/day of butter, another as each 5 g/day of plant oil, and the contrast could not be settled. Butter is a food, and plant oils differ from butter in more than saturated fat, so this is not a pure saturated-fat comparison. The two studies use different units and designs, and the reason they differ was not established.

### 6.2 Meat

In the UK Biobank (474,985 adults), processed meat was associated with ischaemic heart disease, HR 1.09 (1.04–1.15) per 20 g/day, and unprocessed red plus processed meat with HR 1.15 (1.07–1.23) per 70 g/day; higher body mass index explained a substantial share of the associations (Papier et al., 2021; design V2, estimates V1). A systematic review of randomized trials found that diets lower in red meat may have little or no effect on all-cause mortality (HR 0.99; 0.95–1.03) and cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.98; 0.91–1.06), with low to very low certainty; a companion cohort meta-analysis found very small risk reductions, also low certainty (Zeraatkar et al., 2019; V1). In a feeding trial, red and white meat raised LDL cholesterol and apolipoprotein B relative to non-meat protein regardless of saturated-fat level, while higher saturated fat raised them regardless of protein source (Bergeron et al., 2019; V1). The International Agency for Research on Cancer classes processed meat as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) and red meat as probably carcinogenic (Group 2A), a statement about the strength of evidence, not the size of risk (Bouvard et al., 2015; V2). The Astrup critique argues that unprocessed red meat is not linked to heart disease and that its saturated-fat content is "unlikely to be responsible" for risk, which sits against the UK Biobank and trial results above. Which meat components drive risk (saturated fat, sodium, nitrite, heme iron) was **NOT SEARCHED** `[MATERIAL GAP]`.

### 6.3 Tropical oils and individual fatty acids

Coconut oil raised LDL cholesterol by 10.47 mg/dL relative to non-tropical vegetable oils across 17 trials, and raised HDL cholesterol by 4.00 mg/dL (Neelakantan et al., 2020; Table 3). It did not differ from olive oil in one small trial whose interval could not rule out a difference of about 0.2 mmol/L either way (Khaw et al., 2018). Palm oil, palm kernel oil, medium-chain triglyceride oil, cocoa butter and stearic acid, very-long-chain saturated fatty acids and the claim that pentadecanoic acid is an "essential" fatty acid were **NOT SEARCHED** `[MATERIAL GAP]`. For individual fatty acids, two cohort summaries are available: higher intakes of lauric, myristic, palmitic and stearic acid were associated with 7%, 13%, 18% and 18% higher CHD incidence in the two Harvard cohorts, while replacing 1% of energy from these acids with polyunsaturated fat, monounsaturated fat or whole grains went with 8%, 5% and 6% lower CHD (Zong et al., 2016; V1, exposure contrast not displayed); and in a type 2 diabetes meta-analysis higher myristic and lauric acid intakes were inversely associated with incidence (V1). These point in different directions for the same acids, and the outcomes differ, so they should not be combined.

### 6.4 Ultra-processed food

An umbrella review reported that higher exposure to ultra-processed food is associated with an increased risk of 32 adverse health outcomes (Lane et al., 2024; V1, press summary); no result addressed saturated fat or its adjustment. The 2025–2030 DGA ties limiting "highly processed foods" to meeting the saturated-fat goal (V2), and defines them as foods made primarily from substances extracted from food and containing industrially manufactured additives (V1). Whether processing, rather than the fat, explains associations for foods such as processed meat is unknown from this run.

### 6.5 What the food evidence adds up to

Two points are well supported by the retrieved trials: the same weight of saturated fat can have different effects on LDL cholesterol depending on the food, and every saturated-fat-rich food tested still raised LDL cholesterol more than a polyunsaturated-fat comparator. Whether those differences translate into different rates of heart attacks or deaths is not shown by any trial. The Nutrition Evidence Systematic Review (NESR) concluded, with moderate certainty, that swapping red or processed meat for dairy, whole grains or vegetables goes with lower cardiovascular morbidity; this points toward food-level advice, but it is observational (V1).

## 7. Natural versus artificial

This section addresses the question directly. The honest short answer is that the evidence retrieved supports a clear contrast for trans fat, supports no contrast for the saturated fatty acid itself, and does not reach most other "artificial" fats.

**What can differ between two fats.** Between a "natural" and an "artificial" fat the real differences are the geometry of a double bond (the defining feature of trans fat), the position of fatty acids on the glycerol backbone, the food matrix, co-ingredients and process contaminants. Only the first and the matrix were covered by retrieved evidence. Position (relevant to interesterification), contaminants from refining and heating, and molecular identity were **NOT SEARCHED**.

### 7.1 Same fatty acid, different origin (R-a): NOT SEARCHED

No retrieved result addresses whether a saturated fatty acid made in a factory behaves differently from the same fatty acid in butter. The retrieved trials point to the replacement nutrient and the food as the sources of variation (Sections 4 and 6), and nothing suggests origin matters. This is absence of searched evidence, not evidence of absence. `[MATERIAL GAP]`

### 7.2 Industrial trans fat (R-b): the clear case

Trans fat is not a saturated fat. It is an unsaturated fat in which at least one double bond is in the trans configuration (Mozaffarian et al., 2006; V1). The industrial source is partially hydrogenated oil (PHO).

- **Cohorts.** A pooled analysis of four cohorts found RR 1.23 (1.11–1.37) for CHD per 2% of energy from trans fat (Mozaffarian et al., 2006; V1). A meta-analysis of observational studies found total trans fat associated with all-cause mortality (RR 1.34; 1.16–1.56; V2), CHD mortality (1.28; 1.09–1.50) and total CHD (1.21; 1.10–1.33) (de Souza et al., 2015; V1). Industrial trans fat had CHD RR 1.42 (1.05–1.92) and CHD mortality 1.18 (1.04–1.33); a separate earlier meta-analysis found the industrial estimate not statistically significant, RR 1.21 (0.97–1.50) (Bendsen et al., 2011; V1).
- **Feeding trials.** In a quantitative review of 39 studies, each 1% of energy from industrial trans fat replacing cis-monounsaturated fat raised the LDL:HDL ratio by 0.053, after a published correction from 0.055; the authors said the correction did not change their conclusion (Brouwer et al., 2010; original V2, corrected V1).
- **Regulation and population effects.** The FDA determined in June 2015 that partially hydrogenated oils are not generally recognised as safe, set 18 June 2018 as the general compliance date and 1 January 2021 as the final one, and issued a rule effective 22 December 2023 revoking remaining uses (Food and Drug Administration, 2015; V2). The WHO attributes more than 278,000 deaths a year to industrial trans fat and reported best-practice policies in 53 countries (3.7 billion people, 46% of the world) at the end of 2023; nine countries had been validated for elimination by May 2025 (World Health Organization, 2024; V2). Population evidence is mixed in design and result: Denmark's 2004 limit was followed by about 14.2 fewer CVD deaths per 100,000 per year relative to a synthetic control (Restrepo & Rieger, 2016; V1), New York counties with restrictions saw a 6.2% greater decline in heart attack and stroke admissions (Brandt et al., 2017; V1), and Austria's 2009 regulation showed no significant change in the CVD mortality trend (V1; cited by description in the reference list). All three are ecological comparisons.

Certainty that industrial trans fat is harmful: **high**. No evidence of benefit from it was found.

### 7.3 Natural (ruminant) trans fat (R-d): "natural" does not mean "neutral"

- **Cohorts.** Ruminant trans fat was not significantly associated with CHD: RR 0.93 (0.73–1.18) for CHD and 1.01 (0.71–1.43) for CHD mortality (de Souza et al., 2015), and RR 0.92 (0.76–1.11) in an earlier meta-analysis (Bendsen et al., 2011), all V1. The intervals are wide, so modest harm is not excluded. Circulating trans-palmitoleic acid, a dairy-fat marker, was inversely associated with new-onset diabetes in a cohort of 3,736 older adults with 304 cases (hazard ratios 0.41 and 0.38 in upper fifths; the exact contrast was not displayed; V1), but a biomarker association does not show that eating it is protective.
- **Feeding trials.** The same trials suggest the lipid effect is similar per gram. Across the quantitative review, ruminant and industrial trans fat did not differ significantly (p = 0.37), and a later review by the same group concluded that "gram for gram, all trans fatty acids have largely the same effect on blood lipoproteins" (Brouwer et al., 2010, V2 for the original; Brouwer et al., 2013, V1). In a double-blind trial of 106 adults, 3.3% of energy as vaccenic acid (the main ruminant trans fat) raised LDL cholesterol, total cholesterol and apolipoprotein B by 2% to 6% relative to a stearic-acid control diet, as industrial trans fat did, and also raised HDL cholesterol, apolipoprotein A-I and lipoprotein(a) (Gebauer et al., 2015; numbers V1, qualitative result V2). An earlier trial (TRANSFACT) reported that the HDL-lowering effect was specific to industrial sources, and that LDL rose with natural sources in women but not in men (Chardigny et al., 2008; V1). A meta-regression of 13 trials found no linear relationship between ruminant trans fat and lipid ratios over 0.1% to 4.2% of energy (Gayet-Boyer et al., 2014; V1, funding not displayed).
- **Industry funding.** A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis (10 trials; 12 cohorts) concluded that trans fats from dairy foods do not affect cardiometabolic disease risk. It was funded by the US National Dairy Council, two authors are affiliated with dairy bodies, and a corrigendum exists whose content was not read (Gayet-Boyer et al., 2026; funding V2). Its numbers should be treated as provisional.
- **Dose.** The trial dose (3.3% of energy) is above usual ruminant intakes. The WHO limit of less than 1% of energy applies to industrial *and* ruminant trans fat together (V2).

Certainty that ruminant trans fat is lipid-neutral: **low, leaning against**. Certainty that it causes population harm at usual intakes: **low and unresolved**.

### 7.4 Interesterified, fully hydrogenated and refined fats; fat substitutes (R-b and R-c): NOT SEARCHED

These were not reached. The topics to check, and the questions they pose, are in `resume_plan.md`: whether chemical or enzymatic interesterification of palmitic- and stearic-rich fats changes lipids or blood glucose; whether fully hydrogenated fat (mostly stearic acid) behaves like other saturated fat; what is known of olestra, salatrim and diacylglycerol oil; and how process contaminants (3-MCPD, glycidyl esters) and frying products relate to health. `[MATERIAL GAP]`

### 7.5 Lab-made and cultivated fats (R-c): one regulatory fact

Cell-cultivated pork fat made by Mission Barns received an FDA "no further questions" response and US Department of Agriculture clearance, and products reached the US market on 24 July 2025 (existence V2). That is a safety-status clearance, not a nutrition study. No data on the cardiometabolic effects of cultivated fat were seen, and whether its fatty-acid composition matches conventional pork fat was not displayed. Fermentation-derived and CO2-derived fats were **NOT SEARCHED**.

### 7.6 Whole food versus processed (R-e)

Section 6 covers what was retrieved. In short, a "natural" whole food can still raise LDL cholesterol (butter, tallow, coconut oil), and a processed product can carry the same fat. The US guidelines' distinction between whole-food and highly processed sources of fat is policy language; the evidence behind it, beyond the observational NESR conclusions, was not retrieved.

### 7.7 "Natural" animal fats versus "seed oils" (R-f)

Popular framing sets butter, tallow and coconut oil (natural) against soybean, canola and sunflower oils (industrial). The retrieved evidence does not follow that line. The natural animal and tropical fats raised LDL cholesterol relative to unsaturated oils in trials (Table 3), and a 2026 cardiology clinician's guide states beef tallow is "consistently linked to increased LDL cholesterol versus unsaturated oils" and places seed oils among items with evidence of benefit on cardiovascular outcomes (Miller et al., 2026; V1, wording from a machine summary). Plant-oil intake went with lower mortality in US cohorts (Zhang et al., 2025). Against that, the HHS Secretary has said seed oils are one of the driving causes of the obesity epidemic (V1); a fact-checking organisation called the FDA commissioner's related concerns about seed oils in infant formula unsubstantiated (V1); Louisiana passed a law on label disclosure and school meals covering more than 40 ingredients, seed oils among them; and Texas requires warning labels on foods containing any of 44 ingredients, including partially hydrogenated oil (V1). The primary literature on linoleic acid, inflammation and cardiovascular outcomes, and the contrary reanalyses of the Sydney and Minnesota trials, were **NOT SEARCHED**, so this section cannot say whether claims about seed oils have scientific support beyond the few items above. `[MATERIAL GAP]`

## 8. Controversy, funding and bias

**History and framing.** An analysis of internal documents showed that the Sugar Research Foundation sponsored its first coronary heart disease research project in 1965, paying the Harvard scientists the equivalent of about $48,000 to $50,000 in 2016 dollars (the two search outputs disagree on the figure), and that the scientists did not disclose the funding; the authors found no direct evidence that the industry wrote or changed the review (Kearns et al., 2016; V2). This documents industry influence on how the debate was framed. It does not show that saturated fat is harmful or harmless.

**The two sides of the current debate.** The Astrup and colleagues JACC review argues that meta-analyses show no benefit of reducing saturated fat for cardiovascular disease and total mortality, that the rise in LDL cholesterol is in larger LDL particles which the authors say are much less strongly related to cardiovascular risk, and it proposes food-based rather than nutrient-based recommendations (V2). The AHA presidential advisory argues the opposite, with the benefit appearing when polyunsaturated fat replaces saturated fat and no benefit when refined carbohydrate does (V1). A structured debate in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* had Kris-Etherton arguing for and Krauss against reducing saturated fat as much as possible (V1). Neither document's displayed text shows the trial sets behind its claim, so the headline conflict cannot be traced to specific trials from this run.

**Declared interests.** The Astrup review's disclosure section, as summarised, lists research funding or honoraria from dairy bodies (Danish Dairy Foundation, Arla Foods, European Milk Foundation, Dairy Management, National Dairy Council, Dairy Farmers of Canada), the National Cattlemen's Beef Association and the Malaysian Palm Board, and commercial ties to ketogenic-diet companies; three authors declared nothing (V1, to be re-checked against the journal). Declared relationships are not evidence about whether a conclusion is right. The disclosures of the authors of the opposing documents were not retrieved, so this record is one-sided by search coverage, not by finding.

**Trial meta-analyses that argue against the guidelines.** One reanalysis of six trials from before 1983 (2,467 men) found cholesterol fell significantly more in the intervention groups with no significant difference in CHD or all-cause mortality (Harcombe et al., 2015; V2). A later meta-analysis of 10 trials and 62,421 participants reported all-cause mortality RR 0.991 (0.935–1.051) (authorship not confirmed from the search output; V1). Both test dietary-fat advice, not saturated fat swapped for polyunsaturated fat, so they are not directly comparable to the trials above that replaced saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat.

**Other disputes.** A BMJ investigative article criticising the 2015 US advisory committee drew a petition from more than 180 scientists alleging 11 errors; the BMJ corrected or clarified seven and declined to retract (V1, the petitioners' account). Two meta-research studies found industry-sponsored nutrition articles more likely to report favourable conclusions: odds ratio 7.61 (1.27–45.73) in one and RR 1.31 (0.99–1.72, not significant) in a systematic review of 12 reports; neither is specific to saturated fat (V1). A widely repeated claim that the AHA received funds from a consumer-goods company in 1948 appeared only in blog and advocacy sources and is not used.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 What is established, contested and unknown

| | Statement |
|---|---|
| **Established (high certainty)** | Replacing butter, tallow or tropical oils with unsaturated plant oils lowers LDL cholesterol. Industrial trans fat harms cardiovascular health. US and professional guidance diverged sharply in 2026. |
| **Supported, with qualifications (moderate certainty)** | Reducing saturated fat modestly lowers combined cardiovascular events in trials (about 17% relative), more clearly in higher-risk people and when polyunsaturated fat is the replacement. |
| **Contested** | Whether reducing saturated fat lowers deaths; how large the benefit is in low-risk people; whether dairy fat is neutral or protective; whether the *Annals* review supports "nuance" or continued restriction; whether ruminant trans fat is harmless at usual intakes. |
| **Unknown (not searched or not found)** | Whether the origin of a saturated fatty acid matters; health effects of interesterified fats, fat substitutes and lab-made fats; liver, cognitive, inflammatory and cancer outcomes (beyond one unrated umbrella review); susceptibility (familial hypercholesterolaemia, cholesterol "hyper-responders", genetics, children, older adults); the causal chain from diet to LDL cholesterol to events as argued by both sides; intake levels and the content of other national guidelines. |

*Table 7. Status of the main claims.*

### 9.2 What this means in practice

This is general information, not personal medical advice. The evidence retrieved points toward four practical conclusions.

- **Swap, rather than only cut.** The evidence for benefit comes from replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat, mainly polyunsaturated. Replacing it with refined carbohydrate shows no benefit in the sources that addressed it. The AHA's 2026 guidance expresses this as using non-tropical plant oils in place of animal fats and tropical oils.
- **The food counts.** Cheese raised LDL cholesterol less than butter in trials; processed meat is consistently linked to harm; the label "natural" does not predict the LDL response.
- **Avoid industrial trans fat; the natural kind is a smaller, not a zero, issue.** Check ingredient lists for partially hydrogenated oils where they are still permitted. Ruminant trans fat in usual amounts is a minor contributor, but the WHO limit covers it too.
- **Risk level changes the stakes.** In the *Annals* review the benefit was concentrated in people at high cardiovascular risk and was small in people at low risk. People with established heart disease, high LDL cholesterol or other risk factors are the group for whom trial benefit was clearest, and are the people to discuss diet with a clinician.

### 9.3 Limitations of this review

1. **Search and access.** The shared allowance of 200 web searches ended every stream early, and full texts could not be fetched.
2. **Machine summaries.** Every number is a machine-written summary of an abstract or page. The summaries blend pages, sometimes echo numbers placed in the query, and in several cases attached figures to the wrong paper (for example, 12 trials and 53,758 participants were attached to the *Annals* review but belong to the 2020 Cochrane review). Such outputs were discarded.
3. **Labels.** V1 and V2 measure transcription fidelity, not independent confirmation; consensus statements reach V2 more easily than critiques.
4. **Unsearched foundations.** The foundational lipid meta-analyses, genetic evidence on LDL cholesterol, landmark trials and several outcome domains were not searched.
5. **Overlapping cohorts.** Several US cohort papers share the same participants.
6. **Recency weighting.** The December 2025 *Annals* review and January–March 2026 policy items were found easily; older work was reached less often.
7. **Incomplete conflict-of-interest accounting.** Funding statements were not displayed for most sources.
8. **AI assistance.** Nine AI agents searched and extracted; one AI session synthesised and drafted. A human expert has not reviewed the report.

## 10. Gaps and next steps

Coverage by sub-question is mapped in Appendix A. In priority order, the gaps that most change the answer to the original question are: (1) the unsearched "artificial" fats (interesterification, hydrogenation, fat substitutes, refining contaminants, fermentation-derived fats, and the molecular-identity question); (2) the foundations of the cholesterol argument (feeding-trial meta-analyses and the genetic and drug-trial case for LDL cholesterol); (3) landmark diet–heart trials and their reanalyses; (4) liver fat, cognition, inflammation and cancer; (5) other national and WHO guidance; (6) susceptibility and low-carbohydrate diets; (7) primary literature on seed oils; and (8) verification of the load-bearing numbers against abstracts and symmetric funding checks.

To finish, three changes outside this session are needed, and they are the user's to make: raise the `CLAUDE_CODE_MAX_WEB_SEARCHES_PER_SESSION` setting (an environment variable, set under the cloud environment's Edit menu, picked up by a new session); optionally allow the scholarly hosts that were blocked so full abstracts can be read; and start a new session in this repository pointing at `research-reports/saturated-fat-2026-10-01/resume_plan.md`. The streams' own estimates add up to roughly 250 to 350 further searches.

## 11. Conclusion

On the evidence retrieved, "saturated fat is bad for you" and "saturated fat is fine" are both oversimplifications. Replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat lowers LDL cholesterol, and in trials modestly lowers combined cardiovascular events, with the benefit concentrated in higher-risk people and with polyunsaturated fat as the replacement. The effect on deaths is not distinguishable from none. What the fat comes in matters: cheese and butter differ, processed meat is worse than its saturated fat would predict, and "natural" is not a useful predictor of LDL response. On the question of natural versus artificial, the clear finding is about trans fat: the industrial form is harmful, the natural form is not neutral gram for gram but is rarely eaten in doses that show population harm, and the largest industry-funded review in its favour should be treated as provisional. Whether a saturated fatty acid of industrial origin differs from the same fatty acid in nature, and whether interesterified, fully hydrogenated, substitute and lab-made fats differ in their health effects, remain open in this run because they were not searched. The interim answer is conditional and incomplete by design; the numbers should be re-verified against full texts before anyone relies on them.
